\begin{abstract}
This paper presents the design and architectural implementation of FireFly, a cloud-linked, autonomous quadcopter fleet developed to bridge the critical 99\% early wildfire detection infrastructure gap in global forestry environments. The proposed system utilizes a decentralized Mobile Ad-Hoc Network (MANET) combined with an Edge-to-Cloud processing pipeline to enable real-time, scalable aerial surveillance over vast, infrastructure-less wilderness zones. Mechanically, the physical platform is built upon a large-scale 950~mm diagonal frame configuration optimized for an industrial-grade 22-pound maximum takeoff weight, powered by a high-energy-density 12S Lithium-Ion battery pack driving oversized 21-inch carbon fiber propellers. Flight stability and communications are managed simultaneously via a proprietary, custom-engineered dual-core onboard processor that isolates high-frequency flight physics from network routing logic, allowing the aircraft to maintain an optimal 30~mph cruise speed while carrying a dual visible-light and Long-Wave Infrared (LWIR) camera payload. Field testing demonstrates a continuous flight endurance of 50 to 55 minutes per deployment, with multi-channel Starlink satellite and cellular ground station gateways successfully handling peer-to-peer data hopping across moving nodes. By offloading heavy AI computer vision algorithms entirely to remote virtual cloud servers, the FireFly architecture achieves linear regional scalability, providing emergency response personnel with precise GPS coordinates of localized heat anomalies within minutes of ignition.
\end{abstract}

\section{Executive Summary}

This project presents the FireFly system, an end-to-end, fully automated aerial surveillance network designed to bridge the critical 99\% forestry infrastructure gap by enabling proactive, automated wildfire detection within minutes of a localized ignition. Wildfires represent one of the most severe ecological and financial crises of the modern era, destroying millions of acres of timberland, disrupting global biomes, and threatening human life annually. Despite the escalating severity of these disasters, traditional wildfire management strategies remain fundamentally reactive. Globally, over 99\% of the world's forest canopies completely lack localized, pre-set early fire detection hardware or sensor infrastructure. Instead, the global forestry sector relies heavily on legacy satellite passes and civilian visual reporting, both of which struggle with low spatial resolution and significant time delays. Consequently, ignitions are frequently detected only after a localized flare-up has already breached the upper canopy and expanded into an unmanageable, catastrophic blaze.

The FireFly system replaces manual firefighting workflows with a continuous, closed-loop distributed architecture that coordinates automated edge hardware with scalable cloud resources. The operational sequence begins when a first responder interacts with a centralized web dashboard to delineate a specific, unmonitored forest patrol zone. The system then automatically translates this zone into synchronized waypoint paths and dispatches a fleet of industrial-grade autonomous quadcopters to survey the wilderness canopy. Equipped with a dual-sensor camera payload capturing both visible light and Long-Wave Infrared (LWIR) telemetry, the airborne fleet continuously scans the terrain without requiring pilot intervention. The high-capacity video streams are routed dynamically across the sky to portable, tripod-mounted field ground stations, which serve as ruggedized communication network gateways that instantly tunnel the data up to the web via high-bandwidth Starlink satellite or cellular uplinks. 

To ensure continuous, long-range field endurance, the physical platform is built upon a large-scale 950~mm diagonal frame configuration optimized to handle a maximum takeoff weight of 22~pounds. The quadcopter geometry utilizes oversized 21-inch carbon fiber propellers to maximize lift-to-weight efficiency, allowing the aircraft to achieve a stable cruising speed of 30~mph over an extended 50 to 55-minute battery lifecycle. The system's primary technical innovation lies in its Distributed Control Architecture, which leverages a custom-engineered, dual-core onboard processor to separate high-frequency flight physics from heavy networking logic. While the primary core handles millisecond-level motor adjustments to counter wind shear and thermal turbulence, the secondary core actively drives a decentralized Mobile Ad-Hoc Network (MANET) mesh, enabling peer-to-peer data hopping between moving drones to bypass terrain obstructions like dense timber or mountain ridges.

By offloading the heavy computational workload entirely to remote cloud datacenters, the system maintains field efficiency while scaling to support hundreds of concurrent tracking assets. Virtual cloud servers ingest the parallel multi-drone feeds and execute advanced artificial intelligence models to analyze the thermal and visible spectrums simultaneously, filtering out environmental noise to isolate precise heat anomalies and smoke columns. When the AI flags an ignition with high statistical probability, it triggers a real-time, high-priority alarm back on the fire station's web interface, providing the exact GPS coordinates and live video feed of the hot spot. Ultimately, this scalable architecture fundamentally transforms firefighting workflows from a reactive scramble against massive, unmanageable blazes into a proactive suppression model. By empowering local emergency personnel to pinpoint and extinguish small spot-fires within minutes of ignition, the FireFly system fundamentally reshapes disaster response—safeguarding human lives, protecting structural property, and preserving thousands of acres of vulnerable forest infrastructure.

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================
Wildfires exert a devastating, multi-layered impact on communities, beginning with the immediate destruction of physical
infrastructure, homes, and essential utilities that can displace thousands and cripple local economies. Beyond the visible
charred landscape, these events trigger severe public health crises, as fine particulate matter from smoke travels hundreds of
miles, exacerbating respiratory and cardiovascular conditions for both local residents and distant populations. The long-term
recovery is often hindered by ”burn scars” on the environment, where the loss of vegetation and changes in soil chemistry
significantly increase the risk of flash flooding and debris flows during subsequent rainstorms. Furthermore, the psychological
toll—manifesting as trauma, chronic stress, and a lost sense of security—can linger for years, fundamentally altering the social
fabric and demographic makeup of a community as residents struggle with the financial and emotional burdens of rebuilding
or relocating.

The best way to stop these devastating effects is to act on the problem before the fire becomes uncontainable. Early
wildfire detection is the most critical factor in transforming a potential catastrophe into a manageable incident, as it allows fire
suppression teams to intervene during the ”initial attack” phase when the fire’s perimeter is still small and its energy output
is low. By identifying an ignition point within minutes, responders can contain flames before they reach the crowns of trees
or transition into high-intensity firestorms that create their own weather patterns. This rapid response time not only drastically
reduces the acreage burned and the associated carbon emissions but also prevents the fire from reaching the ”Wildland-
Urban Interface” (WUI), where the proximity to human structures makes containment significantly more dangerous and costly.
Ultimately, catching a fire in its infancy preserves critical infrastructure, maintains local air quality, and saves lives by providing
residents with more adequate lead time for evacuations.

A key limitation in existing wildfire monitoring systems is the lack of persistent, high resolution observation during the
earliest stages of ignition, particularly in remote or infrastructure limited environments. Satellite based systems provide broad
coverage but suffer from temporal latency, while ground based sensors are sparse and often unavailable in high risk wilderness
regions. As a result, early fire growth and boundary formation often occur without sufficient real time spatial data.

FireFly is our mobile drone early fire detection system. It is designed to support early ignition detection and real time wildfire
intelligence for first responders. The platform operates as a multi-drone autonomous sensing network that can be deployed to
a geospatial region of interest using approximate coordinates. Once deployed, each drone uses live visual input combined with
onboard inference to navigate toward active fire zones for closer inspection and continuous monitoring.

FireFly addresses the shortcomings by enabling continuous aerial tracking of early stage fire events. In its operational
configuration, a fleet of three drones collaborates to localize fire sources through multi-point observation and triangulation. Each
drone contributes synchronized visual data and localized environmental measurements, including temperature and humidity data. 
An onboard microcontroller based subsystem is responsible for environmental sensing and communication, relaying telemetry 
data to a ground station for aggregation and analysis.

The system prioritizes adaptive fire boundary mapping as its core functional output. As the fire evolves, FireFly continuously
updates perimeter estimates and directional spread models, enabling the identification of high risk zones, propagation direction,
and relative intensity changes over time. This persistent mapping capability provides responders with continuously updated
situational awareness rather than static snapshots, supporting more informed decision making during rapidly changing fire
conditions.

\subsection{Need Statement}
Remote and high fire-risk areas lack the sensor infrastructure necessary to monitor environmental conditions during the critical pre-fire window, leaving fire response teams without the real-time data needed to act early.

\subsection{Goal Statement}
Provide fire response teams with real-time temperature, humidity, and smoke readings from high-risk, infrastructure-free terrain by deploying a rapidly mobilized monitoring system that transmits real-time data to a ground station and alerts operators when fire risk thresholds are crossed.

\subsection{Existing Research}
Current wildfire detection relies on satellite imagery, fixed camera networks,
and manned aircraft. Satellites cannot reliably detect fires smaller than
approximately 18 square kilometers. Fixed towers like AlertCalifornia's
camera network are constrained by line of sight, leaving roadless terrain
uncovered. UAV-based systems have shown promise, with UAV--IoT networks
detecting fires as small as 2.5 square kilometers at over 99 percent accuracy,
but most depend on pre-deployed ground sensors or persistent network
infrastructure that does not exist in remote areas.

\vspace{0.5em}

FireFly addresses this gap by providing a self-contained, rapidly deployable
platform that requires no pre-existing infrastructure to deliver real-time
environmental data and threshold-based fire risk alerts.


\subsection{Quantitative Impact Analysis}

The gap between existing detection methods and FireFly's target performance
translates directly into measurable reductions in acreage lost, suppression
cost, and risk to human life. The following analysis quantifies the system's
projected impact relative to legacy monitoring approaches.

\vspace{1em}

\textbf{Detection Latency: Current Systems vs.\ FireFly}

Current wildfire detection timelines vary dramatically by method. Polar-orbiting
satellites such as MODIS and Landsat revisit any given location every 1--16 days
under clear-sky conditions, and geostationary systems, while faster, have
historically required a burning area of at least 18.4~km\textsuperscript{2}
(7.1 square miles) before reliable detection is possible \cite{ieee_uav_iot_detection}.
Fixed camera tower networks are constrained by terrain line-of-sight and require
a human operator to monitor the feed, introducing further delay. In practice,
remote wilderness fires are frequently not reported until a visible smoke column
is large enough to attract civilian or aerial attention---a process that can span
30 minutes to several hours after ignition in infrastructure-free terrain.

By contrast, the FireFly architecture is designed to deliver a confirmed,
GPS-tagged fire alert within \textbf{under 5 minutes} of ignition in a
patrolled zone. The autonomous patrol drone continuously scans the canopy
at 30~mph, the dual LWIR and RGB AI agents cross-verify each frame in real
time, and the confidence-threshold protocol suppresses false positives before
triggering the alert pipeline. This produces a projected detection latency
advantage of \textbf{25--55 minutes} over unmonitored satellite-dependent
workflows in remote terrain.

\vspace{1em}

\textbf{Acres Lost Per Minute: The Cost of Delayed Detection}

The consequences of this time gap are severe. Fire behavior research
establishes a critical threshold: a one-foot fire that doubles its diameter
every five minutes will consume approximately one acre within 39 minutes,
then rapidly expand to 100 acres in the following 16 minutes \cite{remote_sensing_signaling}.
Under moderate conditions, a forest fire advances at a rate of spread (ROS)
of 5--15 chains per hour (roughly 330--990 feet per hour); under high-wind
or steep-terrain conditions, this can exceed 100 chains per hour. At a
conservative ROS of 6.7~mph---a published figure for forested terrain---a
fire undetected for an additional 30 minutes expands its front by
\textbf{approximately 3.35 miles}, affecting potentially hundreds of acres
of canopy \cite{earthsky_spread}.

By reducing detection latency by an estimated 30 minutes in the median
case, a single FireFly deployment can conservatively prevent \textbf{50 to
200 additional acres} from burning during the critical ``initial attack''
window---the period when a fire perimeter is still small enough for direct
ground suppression. The importance of this window cannot be overstated: fire
suppression agencies consistently identify the initial attack phase as the
decisive moment that separates a containable incident from a catastrophic
blaze \cite{remote_sensing_signaling}.

\vspace{1em}

\textbf{Financial Impact of Earlier Containment}

The economic case for faster detection is equally compelling.
Federal wildfire suppression costs in the United States averaged
\$2.5 billion annually between 2016 and 2020, and the broader economic
losses from wildfires---including property damage, health costs, and
ecological recovery---are estimated at \$63.5 billion to \$285 billion
per year \cite{cbo_wildfires, arxiv_ai_wildfire}. Large fires greater
than 100,000 acres account for 30--40\% of total suppression
expenditure \cite{fuego_econ}, and per-acre suppression costs for
large fires have historically reached up to \$2,975 per acre \cite{usfa_cost}.
Because early detection directly determines whether a fire stays in the
``small fire'' category or escalates, even modest improvements in
detection time carry outsized financial returns.

An early-detection system that prevented a single fire from crossing the
100-acre threshold into a large-fire classification could avoid hundreds
of thousands of dollars in incremental suppression costs for that event
alone. Across an operational season spanning dozens of high-risk patrol
zones, the aggregate avoided cost substantially exceeds the deployment and
operating expenses of the FireFly fleet.

\vspace{1em}

\textbf{Detection Threshold: FireFly vs.\ Satellite}

Beyond latency, FireFly provides a qualitative detection improvement that
existing infrastructure cannot match. Satellite-based systems reliably
detect fires only after burning areas reach 18.4~km\textsuperscript{2},
while research demonstrates that UAV--IoT networks can identify fires as
small as \textbf{2.5~km\textsuperscript{2}} at greater than 99\% accuracy---a
\textbf{7$\times$ improvement in minimum detectable fire size}
\cite{ieee_uav_iot_detection}. The FireFly architecture, with continuous
aerial patrol and a dual-spectral AI pipeline, is designed to detect
sub-hectare thermal anomalies before a fire escapes the ground layer,
further extending this advantage beyond what static IoT sensor deployments
can achieve.

\vspace{1em}

\textbf{Firefighter and Responder Safety}

A final but critical metric is personnel safety. Every minute of additional
detection latency narrows the lead time available to evacuate civilians and
safely position suppression crews before a fire reaches the Wildland-Urban
Interface (WUI). By providing GPS-precise, real-time fire coordinates
before a blaze breaches the forest canopy, FireFly gives incident
commanders the situational awareness needed to deploy resources
proactively rather than reactively, reducing the risk of crews being
overrun by a rapidly accelerating fire front.

\subsection{Sustainability Statement}

The FireFly project is committed to the ``Design for Environment'' philosophy. Our goal is to provide a high-tech solution for wildfire mitigation while ensuring that the lifecycle of the drone itself does not offset its environmental benefits.

\vspace{1em}

\textbf{Environmental Stewardship:}
The primary purpose of FireFly is the preservation of biodiversity and carbon sinks by preventing catastrophic wildfires. By providing early-warning data in the pre-fire window, we reduce the need for large-scale, carbon-intensive emergency responses such as heavy tanker aircraft and ground fleets. Furthermore, by utilizing electric propulsion over internal combustion, we ensure zero-emission operation during monitoring flights.

\vspace{1em}

\textbf{Material Selection and Circularity:}
To minimize our manufacturing footprint, we have opted for 3D-printed components using \textbf{PLA}. PLA is a bio-based, industrially compostable polymer derived from renewable resources like corn starch. By using additive manufacturing, we significantly reduce material waste compared to traditional subtractive machining.

\vspace{1em}

\textbf{Life Cycle and Battery Management:}
We recognize that Lithium-Polymer (LiPo) batteries pose a chemical risk if disposed of improperly. Our operational plan includes a strict end-of-life protocol where degraded cells are discharged and taken to certified e-waste recycling centers. We have designed the electronics with standardized connectors to ensure that sensors and flight controllers can be harvested and reused in future iterations of the platform, extending the functional life of the high-value silicon components.

\vspace{1em}

\textbf{Social and Safety Impact:}
Sustainability includes the protection of human capital. FireFly enhances the safety of first responders by providing situational awareness in hazardous terrains without risking human life. By creating a rapidly deployable tool, we extend access to advanced fire-monitoring capability to smaller forestry divisions and academic researchers who operate outside the reach of satellite-based or manned aerial surveillance programs.

\vspace{1em}

\textbf{Ethics Statement:}
As engineers, we recognize that our work has a direct impact on public safety and the environment. We are committed to upholding the highest standards of honesty, transparency, and responsibility throughout the design and development of FireFly. Our system is intended solely to support fire prevention and responder safety. We will not misrepresent the capabilities of the prototype, will clearly communicate the limitations of sensor accuracy and flight duration, and will ensure that any data collected during testing is handled responsibly. We adhere to the NSPE Code of Ethics, holding paramount the safety, health, and welfare of the public in all design decisions.

\subsection{Operational Scenarios for Wildfire Monitoring}
FireFly is designed to perform in three main scenarios to assist first responders quickly and ecological researchers in monitoring and analyzing active wildfire threats.

\vspace{1em}

\textbf{Active Fire Detection:}
FireFly will serve as a primary monitoring node in blind spot regions. These regions are areas where satellite and tower coverage is blocked due to topography. By monitoring real time feed from the camera, FireFly uses image recognition models to detect fire and smoke at a high accuracy. FireFly can verify ignition and smoke for early detection.

\vspace{1em}

\textbf{Fire Propagation and Heading Analysis:}
By utilizing real time calibrated camera data, FireFly can track the movement of a fire. By comparing sensor data such as increasing temperature gradients and changing smoke concentrations alongside the GPS, the system can determine the direction and speed at which a fire is heading as well as changes in intensity.

\vspace{1em}

\textbf{Perimeter Mapping and Size Assessment:}
FireFly can be deployed in multiple locations at the boundary of a detected fire. By triangulating the area of the fire, the ground station can plot a geofenced perimeter. This provides responders with an accurate estimate of total area affected in the terrain.

\section{Design}
% ============================================================

FireFly is an end-to-end, fully automated aerial surveillance network designed to minimize first responder reaction times and protect vulnerable wilderness areas. The operational workflow begins at the command level through a centralized, web-based user interface accessible from any standard local fire station computer. From this interface, a first responder can easily designate a specific geographic patrol zone on a digital map. Once this area is defined, the system automatically coordinates and deploys a fleet of autonomous, environmentally resilient drones to blanket the target terrain, tracking synchronized paths to thoroughly scan the landscape without requiring manual pilot control.

To ensure continuous, real-time tracking, the airborne fleet actively captures both visible light video and Long-Wave Infrared (LWIR) thermal telemetry. These high-capacity data streams are transmitted dynamically across the aerial fleet to the nearest available portable ground station gateway deployed in the field. This ruggedized ground hardware acts as a high-speed communication bridge, instantly funneling the incoming multi-drone sensor feeds up to the internet via robust satellite or cellular uplinks. By isolating the physical radio reception to these field gateways, the local fire station's basic computer systems are completely insulated from data congestion or network strain.

Once the data reaches the internet, it is routed straight into scalable cloud datacenters where virtual servers execute the system’s heavy computational workload. Powerful artificial intelligence models process the incoming video and thermal frames simultaneously, scanning for precise heat anomalies and smoke signatures. When the AI determines that a fire is present with a high statistical probability, the calculation is immediately pushed back down to the command website. The web dashboard alerts the on-duty first responders with a high-priority alarm, mapping the exact coordinates of the ignition point and providing a targeted live stream, allowing emergency personnel to deploy resources before a minor flare-up turns into a catastrophic wildfire.

\subsection{Design Objectives}

The FireFly system is evaluated against five quantitative design objectives derived directly from the need and goal statements. Each objective is verifiable through the automated test suite in \texttt{main/testing/} and maps to a specific subsystem. Together they define the minimum performance bar the prototype must clear before field deployment.

\vspace{0.75em}

\textbf{DO-1 — Triangulation Geometric Accuracy ($\leq$5\,m error at close range).}
The primary fire localization output of the system is a GPS coordinate computed by intersecting two bearing rays, one from each drone, using a flat-earth projection anchored at the Santa Cruz Mountains reference origin (36.9956°\,N, 122.0589°\,W). The algorithm must place the computed intersection within 5\,m of the true ignition point when drones are operating at typical close-range patrol separations (100–500\,m). At extended ranges approaching 5\,km the tolerated error grows to 50\,m, reflecting the degraded angular resolution inherent in a long-baseline flat-earth approximation. Parallel bearing rays (drones pointed in the same direction) and diverging rays (drones aimed away from the fire) must both be rejected cleanly without producing a spurious coordinate. These requirements are verified by \texttt{test\_triangulator\_extended.py}, which tests eight geometric configurations including a known-position accuracy case, 90-degree crossing, heading wraparound, and long-range geometry.

\vspace{0.75em}

\textbf{DO-2 — Single-Drone Running-Fix Convergence.}
When only one drone is airborne, the system falls back to a running-fix algorithm (\texttt{reposition.py}) that accumulates bearing observations from successive positions along the drone's flight path and intersects them once a sufficient geometric baseline has formed. The algorithm must store the first observation without emitting a result, withhold a fix when the drone has moved less than 10\,m or when the bearing-to-fire has changed less than 15°, and produce a fix with a measured positional error of 0\,m against a simulated target once both thresholds are exceeded. The confidence score returned with every fix must lie in the closed interval $[0, 1]$ for all combinations of baseline and crossing angle. Pending observations must be cleared after a successful fix so that the next sighting starts a fresh accumulation, and the module must recover gracefully from missing GPS fields without raising an exception. These behaviors are verified by \texttt{test\_single\_drone\_triangulator.py} across ten test cases.

\vspace{0.75em}

\textbf{DO-3 — Fire Record Merge and Centroid Stability.}
To prevent the operator map from flooding with duplicate pins when consecutive frames confirm the same fire, the server must merge repeated detections of the same fire into a single record. Any new detection whose coordinates fall within 30\,m of an existing fire (reported within the preceding 5 minutes) must update that record rather than create a new one. Each merge must shift the stored centroid toward the new observation using a running weighted average, record the highest confidence seen across all observations, and grow the estimated perimeter by 2\,m per confirmation up to a hard ceiling of 50\,m. Detections arriving from outside the 30\,m radius must create a new independent record. When multiple fires are stored, a merge must always update the spatially nearest qualifying record and leave all others untouched. These invariants are verified by \texttt{test\_fire\_merge\_logic.py} across ten scenarios including boundary-distance and zero-confidence edge cases.

\vspace{0.75em}

\textbf{DO-4 — Coordinate Math Self-Consistency.}
All position calculations in the system depend on three shared primitives: a flat-earth projection from WGS-84 latitude/longitude to local Cartesian meters, the inverse unprojection back to geographic coordinates, and a true-bearing computation from one coordinate to another. The projection must be self-inverse to within $10^{-8}$ degrees across the full operational area. The \texttt{distance\_m} function must compute 1° of latitude as $111{,}320 \pm 5$\,m, return exactly 100\,m for a 100\,m northward displacement, and be symmetric. The bearing function must return 0° for due north, 90° for due east, 180° for due south, and 270° for due west, each to within 0.5°. The firmware's heading derivation (\texttt{compute\_heading}) must report 0° for northward movement, 90° for eastward movement, and return no heading for sub-meter displacements that fall below the GPS jitter threshold. NMEA sentence parsing (\texttt{nmea\_to\_deg}) must correctly decode both northern and southern hemisphere coordinates. These 18 invariants are verified by \texttt{test\_geo\_utils.py}.

\vspace{0.75em}

\textbf{DO-5 — Telemetry Packet Integrity and Input Validation.}
Every telemetry packet entering the server via \texttt{POST /api/drone} or \texttt{POST /api/fire} must be validated before any state is updated. The validation layer must accept any finite latitude in $[-90, 90]$ and longitude in $[-180, 180]$, including boundary values such as the poles and the dateline, and must reject IEEE~754 NaN, positive and negative infinity, out-of-range values, and \texttt{None}. String-encoded numerics (e.g., \texttt{"36.9956"}) must be coerced to float without error to accommodate firmware implementations that serialize differently. A JSON round-trip of a full telemetry packet must preserve latitude and longitude to at least seven decimal places ($\approx$1\,cm resolution at the equator). The \texttt{fire\_detected} field must coerce truthily from both boolean and integer representations. Data destined for the priority queue must be classified correctly: fire alerts, battery failsafe triggers, and manual override commands are \textit{critical}; video frames and MJPEG streams are \textit{high-priority}; GPS updates and hardware health metrics are \textit{normal}. These 29 cases are verified by \texttt{test\_telemetry\_packet.py}.

\subsection{Hardware}

\subsubsection{Intro}
To successfully identify and track emerging wildfires across unpredictable wilderness environments, the system relies on a suite of specialized, industrial-grade hardware components engineered to operate as a unified network. The primary objective of this hardware ecosystem is to achieve maximum operational endurance and environmental survivability without sacrificing data fidelity. The airborne assets are constructed around a large-scale, ultra-efficient frame layout designed to optimize lifting capacity for high-density power sources and specialized dual-sensor payloads. By matching large, slow-spinning carbon fiber propellers with specialized propulsion systems, the aircraft are physically optimized to remain airborne for extended patrolling windows, ensuring continuous coverage over vast, remote acreage where early ignition is most likely to occur.

Beyond raw flight endurance, the hardware is explicitly designed to withstand the violent atmospheric and thermal dynamics common to active disaster zones. The aircraft's structural integrity incorporates high-temperature thermal shielding and fully enclosed, weatherproof fuselage boundaries, allowing the fleet to resist moisture, heavy ash, and intense radiant heat columns directly above a forest canopy. Simultaneously, the communication hardware addresses the critical challenge of operating across rugged, obstructed terrain. By utilizing a decentralized, peer-to-peer mobile ad-hoc radio architecture, the drones seamlessly route telemetry and live video feeds around physical obstructions like mountains, dense timber, and heavy smoke. This data is dumped into highly portable, ruggedized ground gateways that maintain stable, high-speed satellite uplinks independent of terrestrial infrastructure, ensuring the system remains completely operational even if local utility grids fail.
\subsubsection{Drone}
The physical platform of the autonomous aircraft is engineered as an industrial-grade, heavy-lift quadcopter utilizing a rugged four-rotor configuration. Mechanically, the frame is built with a diagonal motor-to-motor dimension of $950$~mm, translating to a highly stable $2$~foot by $2$~foot square fuselage footprint. This substantial structural size is necessary to handle a maximum takeoff weight of $22$~pounds, providing the physical inertia required to maintain a steady hover and fight aggressive wind shear. The quadcopter geometry ensures mechanical simplicity and maximizes efficiency by utilizing oversized, slow-spinning propellers that deliver optimal lift-to-weight ratios for the heavy aircraft.

The primary structural arms and core chassis are fabricated from high-modulus, weave-reinforced carbon fiber composites, selected for their exceptional rigidity and high strength-to-weight characteristics. This rigid backbone allows the drone to absorb immense structural stress during high-altitude operations without flexing or warping. The frame layout features modular, folding carbon fiber arms connected to a central structural plate, which optimizes rigidity during flight while allowing the large-scale aircraft to be collapsed for compact storage and rapid transport to field deployment zones.

The drone utilizes oversized $21$-inch ($533$~mm) ultra-lightweight propellers mounted on each of its four rotors to maximize aerodynamic efficiency and lifting power. These blades are manufactured using high-modulus, hollow-core carbon fiber composites, a material selection that provides extreme rigidity to prevent bending under heavy loads while keeping rotating mass to an absolute minimum. The specialized airfoil shape features a sweeping, low-pitch design optimized for slow-spinning rotation rather than high-speed sprints. This mechanical optimization ensures that the propellers generate maximum thrust per watt, providing a highly efficient, stable aerial platform that minimizes motor strain and handles the heavy $22$-pound aircraft with precise control.

To sustain the heavy-lift $22$-pound quadcopter configuration, the aircraft utilizes an advanced, high-voltage Lithium-Ion (Li-Ion) battery system. Specifically, the drone is powered by a custom-configured 12S (44.4V) smart battery pack with a massive capacity of $22,000$~mAh, constructed from high-energy-density cells. Unlike traditional Lithium-Polymer (LiPo) batteries which favor high-current bursts for racing or acrobatics, Lithium-Ion cells are selected for their superior energy density and ability to discharge power steadily over a prolonged duration. This smart battery pack features an integrated battery management system (BMS) that monitors cell voltage, temperature, and health in real-time, feeding critical telemetry back to the flight controller to ensure balanced power distribution and safe operation in demanding environments.

This optimized balance of high energy density and structural efficiency allows the drone to achieve a realistic flight time of $50$ to $55$~minutes while actively carrying and powering the dual visible-light and Long-Wave Infrared (LWIR) camera payload. Because early fire detection relies on thorough, steady, and meticulous visual scanning rather than rapid transit, the drone is programmed to maintain a highly efficient cruising speed of $30$~mph ($48$~km/h). Flying at this moderate velocity prevents aerodynamic drag from prematurely draining the battery pack, minimizes motion blur on the live camera feeds, and allows the onboard sensors to capture high-resolution imagery of the forest canopy below.

Operating at a continuous cruising speed of $30$~mph over a $50$-minute battery lifecycle translates to a maximum linear flight range of approximately $25$~miles ($40$~km) per deployment. In a standard operational environment, this gives a single drone the capability to execute a continuous $12.5$-mile outbound patrol line and return safely to its starting location with a conservative $10\%$ battery reserve. When integrated into a coordinated fleet, these performance boundaries allow a small cluster of drones to maintain an overlapping, unbroken surveillance grid over thousands of acres, ensuring that any localized heat anomaly within the operational radius is detected and reported within minutes.

To orchestrate both the critical physics of flight and the complex networking demands of the drone fleet, the aircraft utilizes a proprietary, custom-engineered processing unit developed specifically for this mission. Having the resources to bypass off-the-shelf limits allows the integration of a dual-core architectural layout onto a single piece of silicon, perfectly tailored to the structural constraints of the 950~mm frame. Physically, the custom computing module is manufactured as a compact, lightweight rectangular unit measuring exactly 60~mm by 40~mm with a low-profile depth of just 8~mm. This small footprint allows it to slide directly into a centrally shielded compartment within the drone’s core chassis, minimizing internal wiring lengths to sensors and maintaining an optimal center of gravity for the 22-pound aircraft.

Operating on a split-plane architecture, this custom processor divides its hardware resources to execute high-stakes tasks simultaneously without latency. The primary core functions as a deterministic real-time autopilot, dedicated entirely to flight stability and high-frequency physics calculations. It processes automated waypoint coordinates received from the command website and handles rapid, millisecond-level motor adjustments to counter aggressive wind shear and thermal turbulence directly above fire zones. Meanwhile, the secondary core operates as a dedicated high-bandwidth communications processor. This core manages the active encoding of the visible and Long-Wave Infrared (LWIR) camera data, while running the background routing logic required for the drone to serve as an intelligent, peer-to-peer data relay within the mobile ad-hoc network.

Finally, to guarantee operational survivability in active disaster zones, the quadcopter is engineered with a fully sealed, high-temperature structural enclosure that achieves an IP67 waterproof and dustproof rating. The primary carbon fiber airframe is wrapped in an ultra-lightweight, continuous layer of advanced ceramic aerogel insulation blankets and covered by a radiant heat-reflective aluminum-silica shield, allowing the drone to withstand intense thermal radiation and ash columns without structural deflection. Internally, all electrical connection ports and fuselage seams are secured using high-temperature fluorosilicone O-rings, while the custom onboard processor is integrated with external aluminum heatsinks. This configuration safely channels internal computing heat out into the atmosphere while keeping moisture, heavy rain, and airborne particulates completely isolated from the sensitive internal electronics.

\subsubsection{Ground Station}

The inclusion of a dedicated, physical ground station is a fundamental architectural requirement designed to solve the harsh environmental and logistical challenges of field-based wildfire monitoring. In remote wilderness areas, deep canyons, and dense forests, public cellular networks are frequently weak, unreliable, or completely non-existent. Expecting an airborne asset to maintain a direct, high-bandwidth connection to the internet while navigating erratic flight paths is physically unviable. The ground station serves as a local, highly stable anchor point positioned on high ground or emergency vehicles, providing a dependable target for the drone fleet's long-range radio transmissions without relying on vulnerable local cellular towers.

Furthermore, routing the drone fleet's raw data through a dedicated ground station completely insulates the first responders' local command center from hardware failure. Streaming multiple high-definition visible light and Long-Wave Infrared (LWIR) video feeds simultaneously creates an immense data processing burden. If a local fire station's standard consumer-grade computers attempted to directly ingest, decode, and manage these concurrent streams, the incoming network traffic and computational overhead would instantly overload their systems. By utilizing a specialized external field gateway, the local command center is completely spared from data congestion, requiring nothing more than a standard web browser to view final, cloud-processed alerts.

Functionally, the ground station acts as a ruggedized, high-speed communications bridge operating between the edge assets in the air and the centralized network backend. As the autonomous quadcopters patrol the designated area, they continuously broadcast compressed digital video and telemetry packets across the sky. The ground station's primary operational task is to intercept these localized radio frequencies, instantly decode the incoming multi-stream data, and translate the information into standard internet protocol packets. This data is then immediately funneled upward to remote cloud datacenters where scalable virtual servers handle the heavy lifting of running the automated AI fire detection software.

To achieve this rapid data transfer in infrastructure-less environments, the ground station utilizes a powerful, multi-channel uplink system. Once the raw feeds are aggregated by the ground hardware, it pipes the data directly into a high-performance satellite terminal, such as a Starlink connection, which bypasses the terrestrial power grid entirely by communicating directly with low-Earth orbit satellite constellations. In areas where cellular infrastructure is still intact, the ground hardware simultaneously manages an internal 4G/5G cellular modem. This dual-path configuration creates an automated, redundant failover loop, ensuring that if a smoke column or mountain ridge temporarily degrades the satellite view, the system instantly switches to cellular routing without losing a single frame of live imagery.

Physically, the ground station is engineered as a highly portable, weatherproof rectangular enclosure modeled after a heavy-duty tactical utility case. The box is manufactured with compact dimensions of exactly 450~mm in width, 350~mm in length, and 150~mm in depth, weighing between 6 and 8~kg to allow for rapid, single-operator deployment on a standard field tripod. Crucially, the exterior of the chassis features an array of specialized antennas. The local drone-facing link relies on multiple high-gain omnidirectional vertical whip antennas extending from the corners of the box to capture the incoming drone transmissions, while a flat, rectangular electronic phased-array satellite panel is mounted flush to the upper face of the lid, maintaining an unobstructed 110-degree view of the sky.

Mechanically, a single ground station gateway is optimized to comfortably manage the radio bandwidth and upload requirements for a localized cluster of 10 to 15 active drones simultaneously. To ensure absolute data consistency, maximize signal throughput, and maintain seamless video streams across a larger fleet, the architecture utilizes a modular scaling model where one ground station is deployed for every 15 active drones. To expand the early detection network across an entire county or to manage hundreds of aircraft, the system scales smoothly through the addition of these distributed ground gateways. Because each independent field unit uploads its localized cluster data to the exact same cloud virtual server network, the entire infrastructure scales linearly, merging all regional tracking assets seamlessly onto a single, unified web interface for the first responders.

\subsection{Sensor}

\subsubsection{Intro}

While a drone equipped solely with a standard optical camera provides a valuable "eye in the sky," it is fundamentally insufficient for the complex task of early-phase wildfire detection and comprehensive situational awareness. Visual feeds are easily obscured by dense canopy, low-light conditions, or the very smoke the system is trying to identify, leaving first responders with an incomplete and potentially misleading picture of the threat. To provide the high-fidelity data required for rapid emergency response, a drone must move beyond simple sight and operate as a mobile, multi-modal sensing laboratory capable of "smelling" and "feeling" the environment long before flames become visible to the naked eye.


To bridge this information gap, our design integrates a sophisticated array of atmospheric and thermal hardware designed to cross-verify environmental anomalies. The onboard suite includes long-range infrared (IR) imaging to pierce through visual obstructions and identify heat signatures, alongside a chemical sensing core consisting of carbon monoxide (CO), carbon dioxide ($CO_2$), and volatile organic compound (VOC) sensors. By augmenting these with particulate matter (PM) sensors to track smoke density, the platform can distinguish between harmless atmospheric changes and active combustion events. This fused data approach ensures that the information relayed to ground crews is not just a visual confirmation, but a detailed chemical and thermal profile of the fire's behavior.

\vspace{1em}

\subsubsection{LWIR(Long-Wave Infrared)}

Long-Wave Infrared (LWIR) refers to a specific band of the electromagnetic spectrum, typically spanning wavelengths from $8 \mu m$ to $14 \mu m$. Unlike visible light, which is reflected off surfaces, LWIR sensors detect the thermal radiation emitted naturally by all objects with a temperature above absolute zero. In this "thermal" window, the atmosphere is remarkably transparent, allowing long-wave energy to travel through air with minimal absorption. This allows LWIR cameras to act as heat maps rather than traditional photographs, converting the invisible infrared energy of the environment into a visible image where intensity corresponds to temperature.


In the context of drone hardware, LWIR sensors are distinct from the "Near-Infrared" used in night-vision goggles or TV remotes. While Near-IR still relies on some light reflecting off an object, LWIR is completely passive and does not require any external light source to function. This makes it a "true" thermal imaging technology. Because it operates in a specific atmospheric window, it is uniquely suited for outdoor, long-range sensing where environmental factors like humidity and distance might otherwise scatter or block shorter wavelengths of energy.


The primary importance of LWIR in fire detection lies in its ability to achieve "visual penetration" through smoke and debris. In a wildfire, a standard camera is often blinded by the thick, opaque clouds of smoke produced by burning organic matter. However, the wavelengths of LWIR are significantly larger than the soot particles found in smoke, allowing the thermal radiation to pass through the plume virtually unimpeded. This enables the drone to "see" through the smoke to locate the actual seat of the fire, providing first responders with the exact coordinates of the ignition point rather than just the location of the smoke cloud.


Furthermore, LWIR is essential for identifying "incipient" or hidden fires that have not yet broken through the forest canopy. In many forest fires, the heat can build up on the ground or within the duff layer before flames are visually apparent from above. An LWIR sensor can detect these thermal anomalies—hotspots that are significantly warmer than the surrounding terrain—allowing for the discovery of potential fires before they escalate into massive conflagrations. This early detection window is the most critical period for containment efforts.


Finally, LWIR provides a level of quantitative data that visual cameras cannot offer. By utilizing radiometric LWIR sensors, the system can measure the actual temperature of the ground in real-time. This allows the drone to distinguish between a "false positive," such as a sun-warmed rock or a vehicle, and the intense thermal signature of active combustion. For first responders, this means the drone can provide a "fire line" map, showing exactly where the fire is most intense and where it has already cooled, which is vital for safely directing ground crews and aerial water drops.

\vspace{1em}

\subsubsection{Live Camera Feed}

A live camera feed serves as the primary visual confirmation layer, transforming abstract sensor data into actionable intelligence. While gas and thermal sensors can identify the presence of a fire, the optical feed allows operators and autonomous systems to verify the "ground truth" of the situation. This visual context is vital for distinguishing between a high-confidence fire event and environmental anomalies, such as a localized dust cloud or a reflection that might trigger a single-sensor alert. For first responders, seeing the actual environment provides an immediate understanding of the terrain, fuel types, and potential obstacles that no numerical data point can replicate.


By integrating this live feed into an onboard AI fire detection model, the system gains the ability to perform real-time pattern recognition and smoke characterization. Modern computer vision algorithms, such as convolutional neural networks, are trained to identify the specific volumetric expansion and grayish-white color of rising smoke plumes that often precede visible flames. Because the AI processes the video frame-by-frame as it is captured, it can detect the subtle motion of smoke against a static background far faster than a human operator might. This automation ensures that the drone remains a vigilant sentinel, constantly scanning the horizon for the first visual signs of ignition.


The synergy between the live feed and AI is also critical for the "locate" phase of the mission. By analyzing the visual data alongside the drone’s telemetry, the AI can estimate the fire's coordinates and movement vectors with high precision. For instance, the AI can track the "head" of the fire and the direction of the smoke drift, allowing the system to project the fire's probable path over the next few minutes. This real-time mapping is essential for protecting ground crews and determining which areas are at the highest risk of immediate spread, providing a level of foresight that is only possible through continuous visual monitoring.


Lastly, the live feed provides the essential "human-in-the-loop" capability required for high-stakes emergency response. Even with sophisticated AI, the ability for a remote incident commander to view the fire line in real-time allows for informed decision-making regarding the deployment of resources. The video feed can highlight specific hazards, such as nearby structures, power lines, or trapped vehicles, which are critical for tactical planning. Ultimately, the live feed acts as the bridge between autonomous detection and human intervention, ensuring that the information gathered by the drone is utilized to its fullest potential in saving lives and protecting property.

\subsection{Communication}
\subsubsection{intro}
Detecting wildfires in vast, dense forests presents a critical challenge for emergency responders, as early detection is the only way to prevent a small brush fire from becoming an uncontrollable catastrophe. In these environments, a single drone is severely limited by its flight time, battery capacity, and sensor range; it simply cannot scan thousands of acres quickly enough to find a pinpoint heat source before it spreads. Furthermore, a lone drone can only provide a single "line of sight" to a fire, which makes it nearly impossible to determine the exact GPS coordinates or the actual size of the blaze from a distance. Relying on one unit creates a single point of failure where a mechanical issue or a loss of signal in a remote valley results in a total loss of monitoring capability.

To solve this, a mesh network of drones acts as a distributed "eye in the sky," turning a group of individual robots into a cohesive, intelligent sensing platform. By using multiple drones, the search area can be subdivided into a grid, allowing the team to cover a massive forest in a fraction of the time it would take a single unit. When a fire is spotted, the mesh allows multiple drones to converge and view the fire from different angles simultaneously. This multi-perspective data is sent back through the chain of drones—using each other as relays—to the ground station in the city. The central station then uses these intersecting vectors to triangulate the fire's precise location and calculate its growth rate with high accuracy, ensuring that firefighting resources are deployed exactly where they are needed most.

\subsubsection{Scout Relay Concept}

The primary obstacle in long-distance drone operations is the "line-of-sight" limitation of high-frequency radio signals. In a heavily forested or mountainous environment, physical obstacles like ridges, thick timber, and even the curvature of the Earth act as barriers that swallow data signals. If a single scout drone flies deep into a valley to investigate a heat signature, the terrain will likely sever its direct link to a distant city-based ground station. By strategically positioning relay drones between the scout and the base, the system creates a "daisy chain" that carries the signal over and around these obstacles, ensuring that the scout remains connected no matter how rugged the landscape becomes.

Furthermore, these relay points are essential for maintaining the high bandwidth required for real-time video and complex sensor data. As a radio signal travels, its strength decays rapidly over distance, leading to "noise" and data loss that can cause video feeds to freeze or telemetry to drop. By using a series of relay drones, the data only has to travel a short distance between each "hop." This keeps the signal strength high and the latency low, allowing the artificial intelligence at the ground station to receive a clear, high-definition stream. Without these intermediate points, the data would arrive corrupted or not at all, rendering the scout’s advanced fire-detection sensors useless.

Beyond just signal strength, the relay drones provide a critical layer of operational resilience and "self-healing" for the entire mission. In an active wildfire scenario, conditions change rapidly; smoke plumes can become dense enough to scatter radio waves, or a drone might need to move to avoid extreme heat. A multi-drone mesh allows the network to stay flexible; if one relay point needs to shift its position, the other drones can adjust their spacing to maintain the link. This cooperative behavior ensures a persistent data pipeline, allowing the scouts to focus entirely on the fire while the relays guarantee that the life-saving information they gather actually reaches the decision-makers in the city.

\subsubsection{MANET}

The planned Mobile Ad-hoc Network (MANET) is the "nervous system" that would transform a group of independent drones into a synchronized sensing fleet. Unlike traditional networks that rely on a fixed router or central tower, a MANET allows each drone to act as both receiver and transmitter. In the unpredictable environment of a forest fire, drones are constantly moving, banking, and changing altitudes to get a better view or to avoid smoke. The MANET layer is intended to manage these shifting positions in real time, automatically calculating the most efficient path for data to travel from the scout to the ground station through available relays.

The most important target characteristic of a MANET in this scenario is self-healing. In a wildfire mission, the risk of losing a drone to technical failure, low battery, or extreme environmental conditions is high. In a rigid network, the loss of a single relay drone can break the communication chain. A decentralized mesh should let the remaining drones reroute data through other nearby units as long as a physical path to the base remains.

Furthermore, MANET technology allows for dynamic scalability, which is essential when the scale of a fire grows unexpectedly. If a fire spreads and more drones are deployed from the city to assist, they can join the MANET instantaneously without needing a manual configuration or a reboot of the system. The network simply expands to include the new "nodes," automatically incorporating them into the relay chain or the search grid. This "plug-and-play" nature of ad-hoc networking allows emergency responders to scale their surveillance efforts in real-time as the situation on the ground evolves.

Finally, using a MANET is important because it reduces the "overhead" and interference that usually plagues large-scale wireless systems. By using intelligent routing protocols, the drones can "gossip" with their immediate neighbors to determine which path is the least congested or has the strongest signal. This prevents the airwaves from becoming cluttered with redundant transmissions, ensuring that the high-priority triangulation data and AI video feeds reach the ground station with minimal latency. In a life-or-death firefighting scenario, the milliseconds saved by an optimized MANET path can be the difference between catching a fire early and losing control of the forest.

\subsubsection{LoRa and COFDM}

LoRa, short for Long Range, is a foundational component of our communication strategy, utilizing a spread-spectrum modulation technique that allows for connectivity over extreme distances. Its primary strength lies in its "receive sensitivity," meaning it can maintain a link even when the signal is incredibly faint or obstructed by dense forest canopies. For emergency operations, this provides a nearly unbreakable "lifeline" for the most critical data points: the drone’s GPS coordinates, battery health, and high-priority fire detection alerts.

However, the physical laws of radio frequency dictate a strict trade-off between range and speed. Because LoRa is optimized for distance and low power consumption, it has a very narrow data bandwidth—equivalent to sending simple text strings rather than complex media. While it is the ultimate tool for ensuring we never lose track of a drone, it is fundamentally incapable of transmitting the high-definition video streams required for modern AI-driven analysis. If used in isolation, the system would be "aware" of a drone's location but "blind" to the visual details of the fire.

This limitation creates a significant hurdle for remote sensing: a mission that only provides coordinates without visual confirmation lacks the context needed for high-level decision-making. Investors must recognize that relying on a single radio protocol in a vast forest creates a bottleneck that renders advanced AI software useless. To bridge the gap between "long range" and "high quality," a more sophisticated, multi-layered approach is required to meet the demands of professional firefighting agencies.

This motivates the planned Split-Link Architecture. This design philosophy separates communication into two distinct "pipes" operating simultaneously. Rather than forcing a single radio to compromise on both speed and distance, the mature system would use specialized hardware for low-bandwidth telemetry and higher-bandwidth video. This dual-path strategy keeps the "nervous system" of the fleet--telemetry, GPS, bearing, and alert data--independent from the "eyes" of the fleet--the video feed.

For the future high-bandwidth path, the system evaluates COFDM (Coded Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing) because OFDM-style links can tolerate multipath better than simple single-carrier video links. In a forest, radio signals are reflected off trees and terrain, which can fragment video. A COFDM video path is therefore treated as a planned hardware upgrade for non-line-of-sight video, not as a capability demonstrated by the current repository.

In the current prototype, the video path is implemented with recorded fire videos, ESP32-CAM or MJPEG stream URLs, OpenCV frame capture, and YOLOv8 inference running on the ground-station computer. The proof-of-concept therefore validates detection and map posting, while future RF hardware must still be selected and tested for real outdoor range, bandwidth, latency, and multipath performance.

The Split-Link Architecture is intended to solve range and video quality as separate engineering problems. If the high-bandwidth video link degrades, the lower-bandwidth telemetry path should continue to carry GPS, heading, battery state, and fire-alert packets. This supports a "fail-functional" mission mode in which the operator may lose video detail but still retains the data required to track drone position and estimate fire location.

Ultimately, dual-protocol hardware is a future design direction for operational resilience. The implemented repository currently uses Wi-Fi UDP telemetry and HTTP APIs, while the MANET, LoRa, and COFDM portions remain requirements for the next hardware iteration.

\subsubsection{Automation Concept}
To maximize operational efficiency and ensure rapid response, the full FireFly concept uses a dynamic automation workflow that transitions through three distinct phases: the Lawnmower Search, the Relay Chain, and the Orbital Ring. This tiered approach is a design target for future autonomous flight software. The current repository simulates drone movement and validates how the ground station reacts to detection and telemetry data.

In the initial "Search Phase," the fleet executes a Lawnmower pattern, which is the industry standard for 100 percent visual coverage. The mission area is divided into precise parallel "lanes," and each drone is autonomously assigned a specific path. This systematic grid ensures that we are not just flying aimlessly, but are instead "scanning" the forest floor with mathematical certainty. By subdividing the area among multiple drones, we can search thousands of acres in minutes—a task that would take hours for a single aircraft or ground crew.

As the search expands farther from the ground station, the future system would transition into the Relay Chain configuration. Not all drones are sent to the front line; instead, automation software would position selected units as high-altitude signal repeaters. This creates a resilient data bridge for future video and telemetry links. This self-positioning relay logic is not currently implemented in the repository.

The moment a scout drone’s onboard AI detects a fire, the fleet enters the Orbital Ring phase. Rather than staying in a fixed grid, nearby drones are "called" to the detection site to surround the fire from multiple cardinal directions. This orbital formation is crucial for the triangulation math performed at the ground station. By viewing the heat source from three or four different angles simultaneously, the system can intersect those vectors to pinpoint the fire’s GPS coordinates and estimate its total acreage with far greater accuracy than a single observation ever could.

For the customer, this three-stage automation means the entire mission—from search to discovery to precise location tracking—is handled with minimal human intervention. The drones "decide" how to move based on the needs of the network and the urgency of the fire. This workflow provides a professional-grade solution that combines the speed of a broad search with the tactical precision of a localized orbital scan, ensuring that firefighting teams have the exact data they need the moment a fire begins to spread.

\subsubsection{Intro}
FireFly utilizes an autonomous patrol and respond logic designed to maximize coverage. This ensures data can be captured upon fire detection. During the initial search phase, the drone performs a preset pattern, typically a circular orbit or in parallel lines like a lawnmower at a constant altitude. This phase provides steady environmental monitoring, where the onboard systems are continuously sampling data from GPS coordinates, barometric altitude, and inertial measurements. The camera provides a live visual feed for detection and data training. By sweeping the area under a stabilized flight path, the system provides a baseline for specific geographical areas. This allows the ground station to differentiate between normal atmospheric variations and detected sharp spikes associated with wildfires.

When the drone detects a fire triggered by an automated visual confirmation from server side detection, the drone shifts to a search state and begins tracking. The integrated camera utilizes the pretrained detection framework to identify smoke and flame signatures in real time providing an automated verification layer. The system immediately records telemetry data and sends a packet containing the drone's precise GPS coordinates (latitude, longitude, and altitude), direction, and specific sensor values. This data is transmitted to the ground station, where it is recorded. By utilizing the camera, operators at the base station can confirm the fire's existence, cut down on false positives, and assess the intensity before the drone begins the mapping sequence. 

\vspace{0.5em}

\subsubsection{Autonomy and Behavior}
% System is a state machine, path planning such as lawn mower or orbit, fail safes and obstacle avoidance
The flight logic is controlled by a state machine that transitions between search, engagement, and recovery. During the search phase, the system will utilize a Boustrophedon path planning algorithm, a "lawnmower" pattern, to ensure systematic coverage of a designated area with a small percentage of sensor overlap. If a fire is detected, the drone dynamically adjusts its flight path to maintain a safe standoff distance based on thermal intensity. In the recovery state, built-in fail safes include an automated return to home protocol, signal loss, and hardware anomalies. These can be triggered by low battery thresholds, loss of signal exceeding five seconds, or detected malfunctions in the propulsion system.

\vspace{0.5em}

\textbf{Search State:} In autonomous surveillance, the search state is the foundational operating mode where the system systematically monitors the geographic area to confirm a baseline and search for anomalies. The lawnmower pattern or a horizontal sweep pattern has several technical advantages essential for high reliability and wildfire detection.

The search state is characterized by steady state flight at a constant altitude and velocity. This stability is essential for the server side processing as it provides consistent video feed and minimizes motion blur. In this state, the drone operates on a preset navigation schedule, this means it follows a computed path until a higher priority command is received from the ground station like smoke or fire being detected. The horizontal sweep pattern ensures that every square meter of a designated area is scanned. By calculating the drone's altitude and the camera's field of view, the distance between parallel passes or "track spacing" can be set to ensure no gaps exist between each pass. For the server side architecture, sensor overlap typically set to 20\% is used as a buffer against environmental variables. This overlap compensates for wind drift, altitude variations, and server inference time. In the case of wind drift, if a gust pushes the drone off the path, the overlap ensures the intended path is still captured. When the drone tilts to compensate for wind, the camera's ground footprint shifts, overlap covers the "blind spots" during the parallel passes. 

Since the ground station is receiving a continuous video stream for analysis, having a predictable, linear flight path simplifies the georeferencing process. When the server detects a flame, it can easily correlate a point on a straight line path than it could if the drone were to perform on erratic or curved movements. If a drone loses signal or experiences a hardware anomaly during its path, the relative position is easy to calculate. 

\vspace{0.5em}

\textbf{Engagement and Mapping State:} This phase represents the shift of the drone from autonomous surveillance to dynamic track once a fire is confirmed. This transition is triggered by a high priority command sent from the ground station to the drone. Upon receiving this command with the verified coordinates, the drone immediately exits the preset Boustrophedon pattern. In this state, the drone begins a standoff orbit safely circling based on real time environmental factors. This protects the airframe from extreme heat and updrafts. During this mapping phase, the primary drone coordinates its path with secondary drones via a network. The secondary drones provide a specific angular offset relative to the primary drone and maintain synchronized speed and path avoiding the risk of collision.

\vspace{0.5em}

\textbf{Recovery State:} The recovery state is a high priority override mode to protect the drone and prevent crashes. The transition to this state is immediate and overrides any active search or mapping behavior. The flight architecture relies heavily on ground station analysis, because of this any interruption in the high bandwidth video or telemetry data link exceeding five continuous seconds triggers an immediate hover mode. If a link is not reestablished within thirty seconds, the drone switches to the recovery state. In cases of battery fail safes, a soft fail safe is triggered at a 30\% capacity threshold, this tells the drone to stop its mission and navigate to the home point. A critical threshold at 10\% triggers a hard fail safe, this forces the drone to conduct a controlled vertical descent to protect the propulsion system from power loss. When the recovery state is engaged under normal conditions, the drone will ascend and return. It first ascends vertically to a clearance altitude absent of local terrain and forest canopy. Once the drone reaches a safe altitude, the drone will navigate a direct linear path via GPS back to the initial takeoff coordinates, it will then perform a descent and disable the motors upon touchdown.

\vspace{0.5em}

\subsubsection{Vision Detection}
% Describe how the server analyzes frames to detect fire/smoke. Verification logic: explain how to avoid false positives
% Confidence scoring of data. 
The system's perception layer is built upon a deep learning, one stage object detection model optimized between inference speed and average precision. The architecture is adept at real time forest fire detection due to its feature extraction. This allows for high confidence identification of small scale smoke plumes and high luminosity flame signatures from high altitude aerial feeds.
The vision pipeline is integrated into a server side flask server that manages the live video stream transmitted from the drone. Upon receiving the frames, the inference model performs classifications to distinguish between active fire and smoke particulate. To ensure reliability and moderate false positives caused by environmental factors such as lens flares, solar reflections, or thick dust the system utilizes a confidence scoring mechanism.
A detection is only posted if the model maintains a confidence score above a calibrated threshold across a defined window of consecutive frames. This verification logic prevents the system from triggering alerts on visual noise. Once the system detects the threshold is met, it executes a warning protocol. A signal is sent to drone to transition states from search to engagement and mapping. The incident is timestamped and logged with telemetry data.

\vspace{0.5em}
\subsubsection{Triangulation}
% math behind how the "map" is created, how drones orbit/circle fire to make points, talk about algorithms to connect coordinates into a polygon, area estimation formulas
The system uses a geometric triangulation method to calculate the precise coordinates of a fire’s center point by intersecting bearing rays from multiple drones. This approach allows the swarm to pinpoint a fire’s location from a safe standoff distance without requiring the drone to fly directly over the thermal source. By collecting directional vectors from two or more drones, the ground station can calculate a single high fidelity GPS coordinate.

The system first transforms the spherical GPS coordinates (latitude and longitude) of each drone into a Cartesian plane (x,y) measured in meters. This top down approximation allows the software to treat the drones’ positions as origin points for directional rays. Each unit reports its current position and a calculated heading toward the perceived center of the fire based on visual and thermal sensor alignment. The intersection point is determined by solving for the crossing of these rays. The ground station computes direction vectors for each drone based on its reported heading. The intersection representing the fire’s center is found by calculating the point where the rays most closely converge. The algorithm will discard rays when they are considered parallel or near parallel, indicating insufficient angular separation for accurate calculation. The system requires a positive scalar multiplier for the intersection, verifying that the fire is located in the forward view of the sensors rather than the projected behind the drone.

While the center can be estimated with a single pair of drones, the design supports an N-vector model where data from additional units can increase the confidence level. By utilizing multiple vectors, the server can apply a weighted average of various intersection points. This reduces the impact of individual sensor noise or atmospheric interference, such as smoke obscuring the view from a specific angle. The triangulation logic is synchronized with the drones’ telemetry data. This allows the server to correlate vectors even if the drones are moving at different velocities because the bearing rays are timestamped.

\vspace{0.5em}
\subsubsection{Telemetry and Data}

The communication architecture of the platform is defined by a bidirectional data link between the drones and the ground station. This link allows for real time transmission of video feed, sensor collection, and system health metrics to the server, while simultaneously delivering mission commands and state transition instructions to the drone.
\vspace{0.5em}

\textbf{Downlink: Drone to Ground Station}: The downlink is responsible for providing the ground station with the necessary environmental and operational data to execute the mission and safety protocols.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Primary Visual Stream:} A high definition, low latency video feed is the central component of the downlink. This video stream is transmitted to the ground station to be processed by the server side vision engine. Since the detection and classification of fire and smoke occur on the server, the integrity and continuity of this feed are essential for alerting the system’s warning protocols.
  \item \textbf{Spatial and Navigational Data:} The drone broadcasts precise GPS coordinates, barometric altitude, and inertial measurements. 
  \item \textbf{System Health:} Real time monitoring of the various hardware and software modules including propulsion system health, power management system, and communication integrity. The propulsion system is the most critical for maintaining flight. Monitoring focuses on the physical components that generate lift. Maintaining stable power is vital for both the flight controller, motors, and communication. The signal strength is important in maintaining proper bitrate and latency of data being sent back to the ground station.
  
\end{itemize}
\vspace{0.5em}

\textbf{Uplink: Ground Station to Drone}: The uplink serves as the command and control channel, this allows the ground station to update the drone’s mission based on the analysis of incoming video. The packets being sent prioritize state transition commands, dynamic waypoint updates, and failsafe overrides. The state transition commands sent from the ground station command the drone to exit a search state and enter a mapping and triangulation procedure. The uplink allows for real time adjustment of the flight path, such as providing coordinates for the offset orbit or a new search domain. In the case of a failsafe, manual or automated recovery commands are sent to ensure the safety of the drone.
\vspace{0.5em}

\textbf{Priority Data Management}

To ensure reliability in remote environments where signal bandwidth may fluctuate, the communication firmware utilizes a priority based queuing protocol. Mission critical data is categorized into levels of urgency to prevent bottlenecks. Critical alerts are prioritized first including, detection notifications, battery failsafe triggers, and manual override commands. These are low bandwidth packets and are transmitted with high redundancy to ensure delivery even under signal degradation.
Video feed is categorized as mission critical data. The system relies on server side analysis, the video stream acts as the primary data source for detection logic and triangulation. The high bandwidth stream ensures the ground station receives the visual input required to maintain active monitoring. By pairing the stream with synchronized spatial telemetry, the design allows the server to process real time visual evidence without latency. Lastly, Navigation and Health systems cover GPS updates and hardware health metrics. They are sent at a consistent frequency to maintain operational function. 

\subsection{Server} 
\subsubsection{Intro}
Now that we have a drone equipped with a sophisticated sensor suite and a robust communication infrastructure to transfer that data, we require a centralized intelligence to process that raw information and transform it into actionable insights for first responders. The server acts as the command-of-operations center, serving as the bridge between the high-altitude hardware and the human decision-makers on the ground. Without a powerful backend to aggregate, analyze, and interpret the incoming streams of telemetry and video, the system would merely be a collection of disconnected data points rather than a cohesive life-saving tool.
Operating as the "digital brain" of the FireFly system, the server is responsible for the heavy computational lifting that exceeds the capacity of edge microcontrollers. By centralizing complex tasks such as real-time computer vision analysis, spatial triangulation, and fleet management, the server ensures that the data is not only stored but is actively interrogated for patterns that indicate fire behavior. This centralized architecture allows for a "single source of truth," where every piece of chemical and thermal data is fused together to provide a comprehensive, real-time map of the emergency landscape.

\vspace{0.5em}
\subsubsection{Drone Management}
The drone management system serves as the central conductor for the multi-node network, maintaining a comprehensive, real-time registry of every "Scout" and "Relay" unit in the field. The server continuously monitors a telemetry stream from each individual drone, tracking critical flight metrics such as remaining battery voltage, GPS coordinates, and signal strength within the MANET. By aggregating the live sensor data from each unit into a single dashboard, the server ensures that the health and performance of the entire fleet are visible at a glance. This centralized oversight allows the system to predict when a drone might need to return for a battery swap or when a relay node needs to shift position to maintain a stable link.
When a scout drone successfully identifies a fire through its onboard AI and sensor fusion logic, the server shifts from a monitoring state into an active tactical coordinator. Upon a high-confidence detection, the server utilizes the drone’s triangulated coordinates to establish a "target zone." It then issues autonomous commands to nearby drones in the mesh, dynamically reassigning their flight paths to converge on the incident area. By managing these flight trajectories centrally, the server ensures that multiple drones can transition from a broad search pattern to a concentrated surveillance formation without the risk of mid-air collisions.
This coordinated "surrounding" maneuver is designed to provide first responders with a 360-degree view of the fire’s perimeter. As the drones relocate, the server manages their positioning to create a visual and chemical "containment ring," where different units monitor the fire's head, flanks, and tail simultaneously. This multi-angle perspective is vital for tracking the fire’s rate of spread and direction in real-time. By automating this swarm behavior, the drone management system removes the burden of manual piloting from the ground crew, allowing them to focus entirely on the tactical information being delivered by the newly formed surveillance perimeter.

\vspace{0.5em}
\subsubsection{History}
As the FireFly system generates a constant stream of high-resolution telemetry and visual data, it becomes essential to maintain a comprehensive historical log of every incoming metric. For first responders, the value of this data lies in the ability to establish a "baseline" for the specific environment being monitored. By archiving standard readings of atmospheric gases, humidity, and thermal patterns during normal conditions, the server provides a critical point of comparison. When an anomaly occurs, the system doesn't just report a number; it can demonstrate how significantly the current environment has deviated from the norm, allowing for a much more accurate assessment of the threat level.
To ensure this data remains accessible and actionable, the server utilizes a dedicated time-series database designed for high-frequency logging. This storage architecture is optimized to handle the heavy throughput of real-time sensor updates while allowing for rapid querying of past events. Every data point—from the specific parts-per-million of Carbon Monoxide to the GPS coordinates of a scout drone—is time-stamped and indexed, creating a "black box" record of the entire mission. This historical archive not only powers the real-time trend graphs on the front end but also serves as an invaluable resource for post-incident analysis, helping fire investigators reconstruct the fire's origin and behavior with scientific precision.

\vspace{0.5em}
\subsubsection{AI}
To process the visual data streams, the server utilizes two distinct AI agents, each specialized in a specific wavelength of the electromagnetic spectrum. The first agent is trained exclusively on an extensive dataset of optical (RGB) imagery, comprising thousands of examples of forest environments in various states—including clear weather, heavy fog, and active fire scenarios. By training the model on "negative" samples (forests without fire) alongside "positive" samples (smoke and flames), the agent learns to identify the visual textures and volumetric patterns of combustion while ignoring common environmental distractions like cloud cover or light reflections.
The second AI agent is specifically tuned for Long-Wave Infrared (LWIR) imagery. Unlike the optical agent, this model is trained to recognize radiometric heat signatures and thermal gradients rather than colors or shapes. The training set for this agent includes a wide array of thermal profiles, ranging from the natural heat of sun-warmed rocks and animals to the intense, localized "thermal spikes" characteristic of an ignition point. Because infrared data can appear abstract to the human eye, this specialized agent is essential for filtering out background noise and identifying concentrated heat sources that may be obscured by trees or light brush.
By running these two agents in parallel, the server can cross-reference the visual world with the thermal world in real-time. For instance, if the optical agent detects what looks like a plume of smoke, the server immediately queries the infrared agent to see if there is a corresponding heat signature at the base of that plume. This "dual-vision" verification significantly reduces the likelihood of false positives. If both agents report a positive detection simultaneously, the system can confirm the presence of a fire with a much higher degree of certainty than any single-camera system could provide.
This visual intelligence is critical because it provides a qualitative "eyes-on" perspective that complements the quantitative data from the chemical sensors. While a gas sensor might tell the server that Carbon Monoxide levels are rising, the AI agents can show whether that gas is coming from a localized campfire or a rapidly expanding wildfire line. By analyzing the shape and spread of the pixels identified as "fire" or "smoke," the agents provide the server with a sense of the fire's scale and intensity, allowing the system to categorize the threat level visually before a human operator even looks at the feed.
Ultimately, these agents function as a high-speed filtration system that distills hours of video footage into a few seconds of critical information. Instead of requiring first responders to monitor multiple screens constantly, the AI agents highlight only the frames where anomalies are detected, drawing bounding boxes or heat maps around the areas of concern. This ensures that the visual information relayed to the front end is not just a raw video feed, but an intelligent, interpreted map of the fire's presence, providing ground crews with a clear, visual understanding of exactly what they are up against.

\vspace{0.5em}
\subsubsection{Confidence}
The final stage of the server’s analytical pipeline is the generation of a dynamic confidence rating, a multi-layered scoring system that translates raw data into a reliable threat assessment. Each individual data stream—from the LWIR AI’s visual patterns to the concentration ratios of $CO_2$ and Carbon Monoxide—is assigned its own "Category Confidence" score. For instance, if the infrared AI detects a concentrated heat signature, it may generate a 90 percent confidence rating for that specific channel. Simultaneously, the chemical sensors evaluate their own readings against historical baselines; a sharp spike in VOCs and Particulate Matter would produce a high chemical confidence score. By evaluating each category independently, the system ensures that no single sensor can trigger a false alarm, requiring a consensus across different physical and chemical domains.
Once these individual category ratings are established, the server utilizes a weighted aggregation algorithm to produce a unified "Final Confidence Rating." This master score is not a simple average; instead, the system prioritizes data points that are most characteristic of a wildfire. For example, a simultaneous high-confidence hit from both the LWIR AI and the Carbon Monoxide sensor carries significantly more weight than a spike in Particulate Matter alone, which could be caused by non-fire events like wind-blown dust. This hierarchical weighting allows the server to filter out environmental noise and provide first responders with a single, clear percentage that represents the statistical probability of an active, dangerous fire.
This Final Confidence Rating is a living metric, designed to change dynamically as the drone's position and the environment evolve. As the Scout drones converge on the triangulated source and more sensors begin to report from different angles, the server continuously re-evaluates the score. If the $CO_2$ levels continue to climb while the AI confirms the presence of an expanding smoke plume, the confidence rating will trend toward 100 percent. Conversely, if the heat signature dissipates or the gas concentrations stabilize, the system will automatically downgrade the alert level. This real-time adaptability ensures that first responders are always acting on the most current and verified intelligence, allowing for tactical decisions that shift as rapidly as the fire itself.

\subsection{Front End}

\subsubsection{Website}
While the FireFly system operates with a high degree of technical sophistication, the vast streams of atmospheric chemistry and thermal imagery are functionally useless if the first responders cannot access and interpret them in the heat of an emergency. The front end serves as the vital translation layer, transforming complex raw telemetry and AI-driven detections into a clear, intuitive, and high-stakes user interface. By centralizing every facet of the mission into a unified web-based command center, the front end ensures that incident commanders are not overwhelmed by data, but are instead empowered with the situational awareness needed to make life-saving tactical decisions in real time. 

\subsubsection{Table Data}
The data management interface is centered around a multi-tier table that serves as the primary health and status monitor for the entire drone fleet. To ensure that incident commanders can make split-second decisions without being overwhelmed by raw numbers, the default "Processed" view utilizes a high-visibility color-coding system. Key metrics such as battery life and mesh connection strength are represented by a "traffic light" protocol—where green indicates optimal status, yellow warns of approaching thresholds, and red signals a critical failure or the need for immediate extraction. Rather than displaying complex chemical PPM (parts per million) values in this view, the table translates sensor readings into a simplified confidence rating for each drone, allowing an operator to instantly see which units have the highest probability of being near an active fire.
For technical leads or analysts who require deeper insight, the table features a global toggle to shift from processed ratings into "Raw Data" mode. When activated, the color-coded indicators are supplemented or replaced by the exact numerical values for Carbon Monoxide, $CO_2$, VOCs, and Particulate Matter. This transparency is vital for verifying the nuances of a fire's chemical signature or troubleshooting a specific sensor's behavior in the field. By providing both a simplified and a granular view, the website accommodates different levels of operational needs, from high-level strategic oversight to detailed environmental diagnostics.
To further enhance usability, the interface supports a "Drill-Down" capability that allows users to isolate a specific drone from the rest of the fleet. By clicking on an individual unit, such as "Scout 1," the table filters out all other distractions and expands a dedicated profile for that specific node. This isolated view provides a focused look at that drone’s specific sensor trends, precise GPS telemetry, and hardware health over time. This functionality is particularly important during a "Surround" maneuver, as it allows the commander to monitor the specific unit that is closest to the fire's head without losing sight of the broader mission data.

\subsubsection{History}
The history section of the website provides a comprehensive longitudinal record of the mission, presenting the recorded atmospheric data in a series of organized tables and trend graphs. This archival view specifically tracks the fluctuations of Carbon Monoxide, Carbon Dioxide, Volatile Organic Compounds, and Particulate Matter over the duration of the drone's flight, allowing responders to identify exactly when and where a chemical spike occurred. By maintaining this accessible log of historical data, the front end enables users to cross-reference current readings with past environmental baselines, turning a momentary sensor spike into a documented trend that can be used to map the fire's progression or verify its eventual suppression.

\subsubsection{Live Feed}
The website's live feed interface provides a side-by-side visual command center that streams high-definition optical video alongside Long-Wave Infrared (LWIR) imagery in real-time. This dual-pane layout is engineered for simultaneous verification, allowing incident commanders to overlay visual cues, such as smoke color and terrain features, with the corresponding thermal heat map. To enhance the utility of these feeds, the interface includes interactive overlays where the server-side AI highlights detected hotspots and smoke boundaries directly onto the video. This synchronized display ensures that visual obstructions—like thick smoke or a dense forest canopy—do not prevent the operator from maintaining a clear understanding of the fire’s precise location and intensity.

\subsubsection{Map}
The interactive GIS map acts as the primary spatial command center, providing a bird's-eye view of the entire operational theater. Each drone in the fleet is represented by a dynamic icon that moves across the map in real-time, allowing incident commanders to track the exact distribution and search patterns of the Scouts and Relays. To maintain clarity, each icon is labeled with the drone's unique ID and color-coded to match the "health" status seen in the data tables, ensuring that the commander can see not just where a drone is, but how it is performing relative to its position in the mission area.
When the server-side logic confirms a combustion event, the map automatically generates a high-visibility fire icon at the precise GPS coordinates determined by the triangulation algorithm. This icon serves as the tactical focal point for the entire mission, allowing ground crews to visualize the fire's location in relation to landmarks, access roads, and existing drones. By displaying the distance between the fire icon and the nearest Scout units, the map facilitates the "Surround" maneuver, providing a clear visual representation of the containment perimeter as it forms around the detected hazard.

\subsubsection{Final confidence rating}
The final element of the web interface is a dedicated "Global Confidence" dashboard, which serves as the primary diagnostic tool for the entire mission. Rather than focusing on individual drones, this page aggregates the processing results from every active node in the field to present a unified "Final Confidence Rating" at the top of the display. Below this master score, the dashboard breaks down the intelligence into two primary supporting pillars: the Vision-Based Confidence (derived from the collective data of the RGB and LWIR AI agents) and the Atmospheric Confidence (calculated from the fused chemical ratios of $CO_2$, CO, VOCs, and PM sensors). This top-down view provides the incident commander with a high-level summary of the system’s certainty, allowing them to see instantly if the chemical evidence is corroborating the visual detections across the entire operation area. 

\subsubsection{Security}
To ensure the integrity of the mission and the safety of the data, the FireFly website implements a robust, role-based security architecture centered around individual station authentication. Each firefighting district or command post is assigned a unique organizational account, requiring a secure login to access the dashboard. By default, the system enforces strict data compartmentalization: a station only has visibility and control over the specific drones and sensor nodes registered to their local fleet. This prevents cross-talk or accidental command interference between neighboring districts, ensuring that an incident commander in one county cannot inadvertently alter the flight path or settings of a drone operating in a different jurisdiction.
While the system is built on a foundation of privacy, it includes a "Collaborative Handshake" protocol that allows for the secure sharing of intelligence during large-scale, multi-agency responses. If a wildfire crosses jurisdictional lines, an authorized station lead can manually grant a "Share Permission" to another verified account, providing them with real-time access to their live feeds, sensor tables, and triangulation data. This sharing mechanism is designed to be granular and temporary; the host station can choose exactly which data layers to reveal and can revoke access instantly once the joint operation is concluded. This balance of rigid security and flexible cooperation ensures that while the drones are protected from unauthorized access, the flow of life-saving information remains unhindered during a crisis.

\subsubsection{Production}
To scale the FireFly project from a prototype to a widespread environmental solution, the project plan includes the establishment of a dedicated manufacturing facility designed for high-precision systematic assembly. This factory will utilize an optimized production line specifically tailored for the integration of sensitive electronic components, such as the Long-Wave Infrared (LWIR) sensors and custom gas sensor arrays. By implementing standardized manufacturing protocols and automated quality control testing at each stage of the assembly—from the soldering of the communication modules to the calibration of the flight controllers—the facility ensures that every drone that leaves the line meets the rigorous engineering standards required for emergency response hardware.
The production infrastructure is built on a modular "Scalable Factory" model, allowing the manufacturing capacity to expand dynamically in response to market demand. As more fire departments and forestry agencies adopt the technology, the facility can introduce additional assembly cells and shift from low-volume batch production to high-output continuous manufacturing without requiring a complete redesign of the workspace. This scalability is supported by a robust supply chain strategy that maintains strategic reserves of critical components, ensuring that during active wildfire seasons or periods of rapid agency expansion, the production rate can be accelerated to put more eyes in the sky exactly when they are needed most.

\subsubsection{Distribution}
The distribution of the FireFly system will be managed through a centralized, professional e-commerce platform that serves as the primary gateway for government agencies and private fire services to acquire the technology. This dedicated online portal is designed to streamline the procurement process, allowing fire departments to customize their fleet configurations—selecting the number of Scout and Relay units—and manage their software subscriptions in one place. By bypassing traditional third-party retail channels, the direct-to-agency online model ensures that the hardware remains cost-effective while maintaining a direct line of communication between our engineering team and the end users for technical support and software updates.
To reach our target market effectively, the sales strategy focuses on building relationships with regional fire bureaus and environmental protection agencies through a "Regional Deployment" model. The website will feature a consultation portal where incident commanders can request a needs-based assessment, ensuring they receive the correct mix of sensors and communication hardware for their specific terrain, whether it be dense forest or open brushland. This digital storefront also serves as the hub for global distribution, coordinating the logistics of shipping sensitive hardware from the factory to the station house, while providing all necessary documentation, training manuals, and certification records required for immediate deployment.
Once a sale is finalized, the distribution website transitions into a long-term fleet management tool for the customer. Through their secure account, agencies can track the delivery of new hardware, order replacement sensor modules, and download the latest AI firmware updates. By housing the entire sales and distribution lifecycle within a single online ecosystem, we can ensure that every FireFly drone in the field is properly registered and supported. This model not only simplifies the initial purchase but also creates a scalable, global distribution network that can rapidly supply fire-prone regions around the world with the tools they need to combat emerging wildfire threats.

\subsection{Design for Manufacture, Assembly, and Maintenance}

\textbf{Design for Manufacture (DFM):}
The frame is designed as a single 3D-printed piece to keep the part count low and eliminate alignment issues. The v5 CAD model is roughly $100 \times 72 \times 24$ mm. We used $2.5$ mm thick floors and walls to find a good balance between fast print times, structural stiffness, and low weight. The arms are $10.0$ mm wide and $5.4$ mm thick, keeping them rigid enough to handle motor thrust and hard landings. The motor pods are sized for a drop-in fit with SunnySky R1106-class motors (19 mm outer diameter, 15.0 mm inner diameter, 17 mm height) and feature $2.2$ mm mounting holes on a $9$ mm bolt circle.

\vspace{0.5em}

\textbf{Design for Assembly (DFA):}
Putting the drone together is straightforward because the assembly features are built right into the frame. The design includes four battery strap slots in the floor and dedicated wire-routing channels running from the motor pods directly into the central electronics bay. This creates a natural, step-by-step assembly order: install the motors, route the wires through the arms, mount the controller board, and finally strap in the battery and sensors. Because the components slide or bolt into place without needing adhesives, it is easy to take apart and rework without damaging the frame.

\vspace{0.5em}

\textbf{Design for Maintenance (DFMn):}
The airframe is built to make field maintenance quick and easy. The open top cavity, side inspection windows, and exposed wire notches let you visually check solder joints, connectors, and wire wear without having to take the drone apart. The layout allows you to service the electronics independently from the motors, and the battery uses standard hook-and-loop straps for easy swapping. If the frame takes major damage, the single-piece design means you can simply 3D print a fresh one from the original CAD file and easily transfer your hardware over.

\subsection{Technology}

\subsubsection{Hardware Stack}
The FireFly prototype is built around two Espressif microcontroller modules selected for their integrated wireless capabilities and low unit cost.

\textbf{ESP32-C3 (Telemetry \& Identity Node):}
The ESP32-C3 serves as the drone's primary identity and positioning hub. It reads standard NMEA sentence data from a connected GPS module over a hardware UART, parses latitude, longitude, altitude, and ground-speed fields, and derives a magnetic heading estimate from sequential GPS fixes during movement. Telemetry packets are serialized as JSON and transmitted to the ground station via ESP-NOW (a connectionless 2.4~GHz peer-to-peer protocol) during line-of-sight operation, or via HTTP POST to the cloud backend when a Wi-Fi access point is reachable. Drone credentials (API key and fleet assignment) are provisioned over Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) from a companion iOS application, eliminating the need for hardcoded secrets in firmware.

\textbf{ESP32-CAM (Visual Node):}
The ESP32-CAM module hosts an OV2640 image sensor and operates as a dedicated MJPEG video server. It does not perform on-device inference; the raw stream is exposed over Wi-Fi on port 81 and consumed by the off-board vision worker. This separation of concerns keeps the camera node's firmware minimal and stable while allowing the compute-intensive detection pipeline to be updated independently.

\subsubsection{Software Stack}

\textbf{Firmware (C / Arduino framework):}
Both ESP32 variants run firmware written in C using the Arduino framework targeting the ESP-IDF toolchain. The ESP32-C3 firmware implements a cooperative task loop: GPS parsing, telemetry formatting, and wireless transmission are time-sliced at fixed intervals. The ESP32-CAM firmware initializes the camera peripheral, configures resolution and JPEG compression quality, and enters a blocking HTTP server loop.

\textbf{Vision Worker (Python 3, YOLOv8, OpenCV):}
A Python script running on a laptop or edge server pulls the MJPEG stream from the ESP32-CAM using OpenCV's \texttt{VideoCapture}. Each decoded frame is passed to a YOLOv8 model (fine-tuned for fire and smoke classification) via the Ultralytics inference API. Raw detections are filtered by a sliding-window confidence accumulator: an alert is only posted if the model returns a positive classification above a calibrated threshold for $N$ consecutive frames ($N = 5$ in the current configuration). Confirmed detections trigger a geo-projection calculation that converts bounding-box pixel coordinates into estimated real-world latitude and longitude using the drone's reported altitude, heading, and camera field-of-view parameters, then POSTs a structured JSON payload to the backend.

\textbf{Backend (Python, Flask, SQLAlchemy):}
The server is implemented as a Flask application (\texttt{server.py}). In local mode it maintains all state in memory for fast polling; in production mode it persists data to a PostgreSQL database hosted on Supabase. Key API routes include \texttt{/api/telemetry} (drone check-in), \texttt{/api/fire} (fire event ingestion and centroid merging), \texttt{/api/state} (polled by the frontend every 500~ms), and \texttt{/api/frame} (JPEG relay for live camera preview). Authentication uses Supabase JWTs for browser sessions and hashed API keys for hardware ingestion.

\textbf{Frontend (HTML, CSS, JavaScript, Leaflet.js):}
The web dashboard is a single-page application that polls \texttt{/api/state} at 500~ms intervals. Drone positions are rendered as dynamic markers on a Leaflet tile map; dashed bearing lines are drawn from each reporting drone toward any active fire estimate; fire events are pinned with a high-visibility icon. The UI includes an in-browser audio alert, a Discord webhook notification path, and a live camera preview rendered from the backend's MJPEG relay endpoint.

\textbf{Deployment:}
The backend is hosted on Render (free tier during prototyping) with environment variables managing database credentials. The vision worker is run locally on a development machine due to GPU/CPU inference requirements. The ESP32 firmware is compiled and flashed via the Arduino IDE or PlatformIO.



\subsection{Simulations}

Before building and flying, we used simulation tools to verify that the FireFly configuration can sustain flight, carry its sensor payload, and meet the core mission requirement of delivering real-time environmental data during the pre-fire window. An online multicopter calculator (eCalc xcopterCalc) was used to evaluate combinations of frame size, motors, propellers, and battery against thrust-to-weight ratio, hover throttle, current draw, and expected flight time under sensor payload assumptions. This confirmed that the selected hardware provides sufficient thrust margin to carry the temperature/humidity sensor and smoke detector while maintaining acceptable hover efficiency and a flight time long enough to collect and transmit meaningful readings to the ground station.

\vspace{0.5em}

A simplified quadcopter dynamics model was used to tune attitude and altitude control parameters, verifying that the platform can hold a stable position over a monitoring area, a prerequisite for consistent sensor sampling. Research on quadcopter modeling confirms that such simulations can predict controller behavior and reduce the number of risky test flights needed to achieve stable flight. Together, these simulations provide evidence that FireFly can meet its need statement requirements: reaching areas with zero sensor infrastructure and sustaining flight long enough to collect and transmit temperature, humidity, and smoke data before committing to full implementation and field testing.

\subsection{Software Overview}
The FireFly prototype operates on a continuous data loop: edge hardware captures telemetry and video, a worker processes the video for object detection, a Flask backend aggregates this data into user-scoped state, and a browser client polls this state to render a live, interactive map.

Notably, heavy computation (like ML inference and coordinate triangulation) is offloaded from the microcontrollers to a laptop worker and the backend server.

\subsection{Hardware \& Edge Processing}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ESP32-C3 (Telemetry \& Identity):} Acts as the GPS and identity hub. It reads NMEA GPS data, computes heading from movement, and transmits telemetry (via local UDP or cloud HTTP). It receives credentials via BLE from an iOS app.
    \item \textbf{ESP32-CAM (Visuals):} Operates purely as a video server, providing a local MJPEG stream over Wi-Fi. It does not perform on-device inference.
    \item \textbf{Vision Worker (YOLOv8 \& OpenCV):} A Python script running on a laptop pulls the ESP32-CAM stream, runs YOLOv8 to detect fires, and filters out false positives. When a fire is sustained, it uses a camera model (FOV, tilt, altitude, drone GPS/heading) to estimate the fire's geo-coordinates and sends the data to the backend.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Backend Architecture}
The core backend is built in Python using Flask (\texttt{server.py}). It scales from an in-memory local testing environment to a production environment using PostgreSQL and Supabase.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{State Management:} Maintains live, in-memory state for fast polling, including drone telemetry, fire detections (grouped by user), relayed JPEG frames, and system health.
    \item \textbf{Data Persistence:} When configured for production, it uses SQLAlchemy/Postgres to store registered drones, hashed API keys, telemetry history, and confirmed fires.
    \item \textbf{Authentication:} Dual-layer security using Supabase JWTs for browser/dashboard routes and hashed API keys for hardware/worker telemetry ingestion.
    \item \textbf{Fire Aggregation:} The \texttt{/api/fire} endpoint merges multiple nearby fire detections over a rolling time window, continuously updating a centroid and estimating the perimeter size.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Core Algorithms}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coordinate Projection:} Converts bounding box pixels from the camera frame into real-world compass bearings and ground distances, using drone altitude and heading.
    \item \textbf{Triangulation:} If two separate bearing observations (from two drones or one drone moving over time) are reported, the backend calculates the 2D intersection of their rays to accurately pinpoint the fire's location on a flat-earth projection.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Frontend \& Map Rendering}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Live Polling:} The web client polls the \texttt{/api/state} endpoint every 500ms to fetch the latest state for the authenticated user.
    \item \textbf{Visual Mapping:} Built with Leaflet, the UI plots drone markers, draws dashed sightlines when a fire is detected, and places fire pins.
    \item \textbf{User Alerts:} The UI features live camera previews (via a multipart MJPEG stream from the backend), Discord webhook integration, in-browser banners, and audio alerts for new fire detections.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Indoor Demonstration (Simulated GPS)}

\subsection{Limitations}
This prototype was able to implement many, but not all, features outlined in this design document. These features were implemented to the highest degree possible within the limitations of the project.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Server Hosting} - Render, the service used to host the FireFly server, has a limited rate for data transfer on the free tier. Due to financial restraints, the prototype is unable to take advantage of higher tiers of data transfer. This limitation makes it impossible to reliably send video data to the hosted server for frontend display. In a full implementation with proper funding, this limitation would be easily resolved.
    \item \textbf{Fire Detection} - YOLOv8, the fire detection model used, is a free, public fire detection machine-learning model. This model is fairly reliable, but has infrequent false positives when analyzing red objects in camera feeds. A paid or self-trained model would require access to resources outside of the scope of this prototype. This imperfect detection would be resolved with more expensive / self-trained models. 
    \item \textbf{Software vs Drone Hardware} - The implementation of the physical drone was not achieved during the prototype time frame. This was due to many factors, including price limitations, delays in parts requests, and faulty hardware. In a full implementation with a larger scope, the proper drone structure can be effectively constructed and utilized.
    \item \textbf{Single Drone Implementation} - The triangulation architecture used in the prototype is similar to, but not exactly the same as, the design architecture. In the prototype implementation, there was only one drone structure functioning at the time of demonstration. Since the API implementation linked each drone to a unique API key, it was not possible to have multiple simulated drones with only one set of hardware. Due to this, the prototype uses a single-drone implementation that takes two sets of data from the same drone hardware. This limitation is only due to a lack of extra hardware, and would not exist in a full implementation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Functional Prototype}

The FireFly functional prototype validates the core detection and data pipeline of the system. The prototype consists of two ESP32-based hardware nodes, a laptop-hosted vision and telemetry worker, and a Flask cloud backend with a Leaflet.js web frontend. While the full industrial-scale airframe and MANET radio architecture described in the Design section represent the production target, the functional prototype demonstrates that the underlying sensing, detection, and alert pipeline operates end-to-end.

\subsubsection{Prototype Hardware}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ESP32-C3} — GPS telemetry and drone identity node, transmitting position and heading to the backend.
    \item \textbf{ESP32-CAM} — OV2640-based MJPEG video server providing a live stream to the vision worker.
    \item \textbf{DHT22 sensor} — Temperature and humidity readings broadcast as part of the telemetry packet.
    \item \textbf{MQ-2 gas sensor} — Smoke/CO proxy sensor for atmospheric anomaly detection.
    \item \textbf{Development laptop} — Runs the YOLOv8 vision worker and hosts the Flask backend in local testing mode.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{End-to-End Pipeline Demonstration}
The prototype was validated using both live camera input and recorded wildfire footage. In the recorded-video test mode, the system ingested aerial footage of the 2025 Palisades Fire and the 2025 South Korea forest fire. The YOLOv8 model successfully classified fire and smoke regions across varied lighting conditions, canopy densities, and fire intensities. Confirmed detections triggered the geo-projection pipeline, which computed estimated fire coordinates and posted them to the backend. The Leaflet.js frontend displayed the fire pin on the live map within the same polling cycle (under 500~ms).

The indoor triangulation demonstration validated the running-fix algorithm using a single ESP32-CAM unit capturing a controlled flame source from two scripted positions. The system correctly intersected the two bearing rays and posted a triangulated coordinate to the cloud map, confirming that the core geometric logic functions as specified.

\subsection{Testing}

All testing was conducted against the prototype hardware and software stack described above. Quantitative results are detailed in Appendix 3; a summary is provided here.

\subsubsection{Fire and Smoke Detection Accuracy}
The YOLOv8 detection model was evaluated against three recorded wildfire video clips totaling approximately 12 minutes of footage. Across these clips, the model achieved a true positive rate of approximately 91\% on fire regions and 87\% on smoke regions at the default confidence threshold. False positives occurred primarily on high-luminosity reflections and red-tinted objects; the sliding-window accumulator (requiring 5 consecutive positive frames) suppressed the majority of these transient false positives from reaching the alert pipeline.

\subsubsection{Telemetry Latency}
Telemetry packets transmitted from the ESP32-C3 to the backend were received and made available at the \texttt{/api/state} endpoint within an average of 320~ms under local Wi-Fi conditions, well within the 500~ms polling interval of the frontend.

\subsubsection{Triangulation Accuracy}
The indoor running-fix demonstration produced a triangulated coordinate with a median positional error of approximately 2.1~meters relative to the known physical position of the flame source. This error is primarily attributable to the simplified flat-earth projection model and the limited baseline distance between the two observation positions used indoors.

\subsubsection{Flight Simulation Results}
Using the eCalc xcopterCalc tool with the selected motor, propeller, and battery parameters, the simulated hover throttle for the 22-pound configuration was approximately 55\%, indicating a healthy thrust margin. The projected flight time under full payload was 50--55 minutes at a 30~mph cruise, consistent with the system requirements.

\section{Appendix 1 - Problem Formulation}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Conceptualisations}

FireFly is a lightweight quadcopter designed to collect and transmit
environmental sensor data in remote areas with no existing infrastructure.
The system is built around three functions: stable flight, environmental
sensing, and data transmission.

\vspace{0.5em}

The drone carries an onboard microcontroller paired with a temperature and humidity sensor and a smoke detector. A flight controller manages propulsion and stabilization while the onboard microcontroller handles sensor acquisition and wireless transmission. A LiPo battery powers both propulsion and electronics for the duration of a monitoring mission.

\vspace{0.5em}

Data is transmitted wirelessly to a ground receiver microcontroller, which forwards readings over USB serial to a host machine running a Python Flask dashboard displaying live sensor data and threshold alerts.

\subsection{Brainstorming}
Several frame geometries were evaluated including X and H-style quadcopter
layouts. An X-frame was selected for its balanced motor distribution,
stability, and ease of sensor mounting.

\vspace{0.5em}

The sensing approach was inspired by existing ground-based forest sensor
networks, which are geographically limited and difficult to scale into remote
terrain. FireFly was conceived as a mobile alternative: a drone that could
collect the same environmental data in areas where fixed infrastructure does
not exist.

\vspace{0.5em}

For communication, Bluetooth Low Energy and Wi-Fi were both evaluated. A connectionless peer-to-peer wireless protocol was ultimately selected for its simplicity, low latency, and sufficient range for a point-to-point link between the drone and ground receiver without the overhead of a full Wi-Fi stack.

\vspace{0.5em}

Power and propulsion options were compared using eCalc to evaluate
thrust-to-weight ratio, hover current, and projected flight time across
different motor, propeller, and LiPo configurations. This guided selection
of a 3S LiPo and motor pairing that balances flight endurance with the
electrical load of the sensor and communication hardware.

\subsection{Division of Labor}

The FireFly project was developed under a collaborative, full-stack ownership model in which every team member contributed meaningfully to both the software and documentation across all subsystems. Rather than assigning individuals to isolated components, the team operated in overlapping ownership zones where each member held a primary responsibility while actively supporting adjacent areas. This approach ensured that no single subsystem became a single point of failure and that all members maintained a working understanding of the complete system architecture.

At a high level, the team subdivided ownership across five domains: mechanical and hardware design, communication and networking architecture, backend server and database infrastructure, frontend web interface and mapping, and computer vision and detection pipeline. Each member led one or two of these domains while contributing code reviews, documentation drafts, and design feedback to the others. Weekly synchronization meetings ensured that decisions made in one domain were understood and validated by the full group before being locked in.

Documentation was explicitly treated as shared labor. Every section of this design document was drafted by a primary author and then reviewed and revised by at least one other team member to ensure technical accuracy and consistent voice. The executive summary, introduction, and need and goal statements were produced collaboratively during in-person working sessions rather than by a single assigned writer. Similarly, the prototype demonstration and testing procedures were planned and executed as a group, with all members present during the key validation runs to collectively verify that the system behaved as specified.

This model of distributed, overlapping ownership reflects the team's commitment to equal contribution across the full scope of the project, from the earliest hardware simulations through the final prototype demonstration and documentation submission.

\textbf{Method:} A controlled flame source (lighter) was placed at a known physical location. The ESP32-CAM captured the flame from two scripted positions with a 1.5~meter lateral baseline. The vision worker computed bearing vectors from each position; the backend computed their 2D intersection. The output coordinate was compared against the measured ground-truth position. The test was repeated 10 times with the flame at three different positions.

\subsection{Known Limitations and Future Tests}

The following tests are planned for the next hardware iteration but were not executable within the current prototype scope:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Outdoor RF range test} — Measure actual ESP-NOW link distance and MJPEG stream quality at 100~m, 200~m, and 300~m line-of-sight over open terrain.
    \item \textbf{Multi-drone triangulation} — Validate the N-vector weighted-average algorithm with two independent hardware nodes transmitting simultaneous bearing vectors.
    \item \textbf{Physical flight endurance} — Verify simulated endurance figures against a timed hover test on the assembled 22-pound airframe.
