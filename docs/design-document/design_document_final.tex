\begin{abstract}
This document presents the design and proof-of-concept for FireFly, a rapidly deployable environmental monitoring drone for wildfire detection in infrastructure-free terrain.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Executive Summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Executive Summary}
% ============================================================

FireFly is a rapidly deployable quadcopter designed for first-response
environmental monitoring in high wildfire-risk areas with no existing sensor
infrastructure. The system collects real-time temperature, humidity, and smoke
data and transmits live readings to a ground station, triggering alerts when
fire risk thresholds are crossed.

\vspace{0.5em}

The system is designed to support real-time wildfire monitoring and situational awareness for emergency response teams through an aerial sensing network. It combines real time imaging with analysis to detect fire and smoke signatures, localize ignition points, and generate geospatial maps of affected regions. The system continuously tracks fire perimeter evolution to estimate spread direction, growth rate, and emerging risk zones.

\vspace{0.5em}

Upon detection of a potential fire event, FireFly performs multi-drone data fusion to improve localization accuracy and robustness. Each drone contributes synchronized environmental and visual sensor data, including temperature, humidity, CO2 concentration, optical density, and UV/IR-based flame signatures. Data is synchronized across the network to support consistent spatial reconstruction and triangulation of fire sources.

\vspace{0.5em}

The system is designed with redundancy and resilience in mind, allowing continued operation under partial network loss or individual drone failure. Environmental measurements and visual observations are distributed across multiple agents to ensure coverage persistence even under degraded conditions.

\vspace{0.5em}

Multiple drones operate together in a coordinated formation, enabling adaptive area coverage, improved triangulation accuracy, and high resolution mapping of fire boundaries. Drone positioning is continuously adjusted based on detected fire intensity and spread patterns to optimize sensing in high-risk regions. Processed telemetry and summarized fire intelligence are transmitted to ground infrastructure for operational decision support, while raw or high-bandwidth data streams are selectively prioritized based on link availability.

\vspace{0.5em}

Validation is conducted through a staged testing process beginning with bench-level verification of core subsystems, including power delivery, propulsion, sensing, and communications. This is followed by controlled flight tests to evaluate flight stability, data link reliability, and real-time environmental sensing performance under operational conditions. Final open-field testing assesses behavior in realistic environments, including detection of smoke and temperature thresholds as well as consistency of data transmission. The objective is to validate FireFly as a functional proof-of-concept for autonomous, infrastructure-independent wildfire monitoring and data collection.

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================
Wildfires exert a devastating, multi-layered impact on communities, beginning with the immediate destruction of physical infrastructure, homes, and essential utilities that can displace thousands and cripple local economies. Beyond the visible charred landscape, these events trigger severe public health crises, as fine particulate matter from smoke travels hundreds of miles, exacerbating respiratory and cardiovascular conditions for both local residents and distant populations. The long-term recovery is often hindered by "burn scars" on the environment, where the loss of vegetation and changes in soil chemistry significantly increase the risk of flash flooding and debris flows during subsequent rainstorms. Furthermore, the psychological toll—manifesting as trauma, chronic stress, and a lost sense of security—can linger for years, fundamentally altering the social fabric and demographic makeup of a community as residents struggle with the financial and emotional burdens of rebuilding or relocating.

\vspace{1em}

The best way to stop these devastating effects is to act on the problem before the fire becomes uncontainable. Early wildfire detection is the most critical factor in transforming a potential catastrophe into a manageable incident, as it allows fire suppression teams to intervene during the "initial attack" phase when the fire’s perimeter is still small and its energy output is low. By identifying an ignition point within minutes, responders can contain flames before they reach the crowns of trees or transition into high-intensity firestorms that create their own weather patterns. This rapid response time not only drastically reduces the acreage burned and the associated carbon emissions but also prevents the fire from reaching the "Wildland-Urban Interface" (WUI), where the proximity to human structures makes containment significantly more dangerous and costly. Ultimately, catching a fire in its infancy preserves critical infrastructure, maintains local air quality, and saves lives by providing residents with more adequate lead time for evacuations.

\vspace{1em}

A key limitation in existing wildfire monitoring systems is the lack of persistent, high resolution observation during the earliest stages of ignition, particularly in remote or infrastructure limited environments. Satellite based systems provide broad coverage but suffer from temporal latency, while ground based sensors are sparse and often unavailable in high risk wilderness regions. As a result, early fire growth and boundary formation often occur without sufficient real time spatial data.

\vspace{1em}

FireFly is our mobile drone early fire detection system. It is designed to support early ignition detection and real time wildfire intelligence for first responders. The platform operates as a multi-drone autonomous sensing network that can be deployed to a geospatial region of interest using approximate coordinates. Once deployed, each drone uses live visual input combined with onboard inference to navigate toward active fire zones for closer inspection and continuous monitoring.

\vspace{1em}

FireFly addresses the shortcomings of the  by enabling continuous aerial tracking of early stage fire events. In its operational configuration, a fleet of three drones collaborates to localize fire sources through multi-point observation and triangulation. Each drone contributes synchronized visual data and localized environmental measurements, including temperature, humidity, CO2 concentration, and smoke density estimation. An onboard microcontroller based subsystem is responsible for environmental sensing and communication, relaying telemetry data to a ground station for aggregation and analysis.

\vspace{1em}

The system prioritizes adaptive fire boundary mapping as its core functional output. As the fire evolves, FireFly continuously updates perimeter estimates and directional spread models, enabling the identification of high risk zones, propagation direction, and relative intensity changes over time. This persistent mapping capability provides responders with continuously updated situational awareness rather than static snapshots, supporting more informed decision making during rapidly changing fire conditions.

\subsection{Need Statement}
Remote and high fire-risk areas lack the sensor infrastructure necessary to monitor environmental conditions during the critical pre-fire window, leaving fire response teams without the real-time data needed to act early.

\subsection{Goal Statement}
Provide fire response teams with real-time temperature, humidity, and smoke readings from high-risk, infrastructure-free terrain by deploying a rapidly mobilized monitoring system that transmits real-time data to a ground station and alerts operators when fire risk thresholds are crossed.

\subsection{Existing Research}
Current wildfire detection relies on satellite imagery, fixed camera networks,
and manned aircraft. Satellites cannot reliably detect fires smaller than
approximately 18 square kilometers. Fixed towers like AlertCalifornia's
camera network are constrained by line of sight, leaving roadless terrain
uncovered. UAV-based systems have shown promise, with UAV--IoT networks
detecting fires as small as 2.5 square kilometers at over 99 percent accuracy,
but most depend on pre-deployed ground sensors or persistent network
infrastructure that does not exist in remote areas.

\vspace{0.5em}

FireFly addresses this gap by providing a self-contained, rapidly deployable
platform that requires no pre-existing infrastructure to deliver real-time
environmental data and threshold-based fire risk alerts.

\subsection{Sustainability Statement}

The FireFly project is committed to the ``Design for Environment'' philosophy. Our goal is to provide a high-tech solution for wildfire mitigation while ensuring that the lifecycle of the drone itself does not offset its environmental benefits.

\vspace{1em}

\textbf{Environmental Stewardship:}
The primary purpose of FireFly is the preservation of biodiversity and carbon sinks by preventing catastrophic wildfires. By providing early-warning data in the pre-fire window, we reduce the need for large-scale, carbon-intensive emergency responses such as heavy tanker aircraft and ground fleets. Furthermore, by utilizing electric propulsion over internal combustion, we ensure zero-emission operation during monitoring flights.

\vspace{1em}

\textbf{Material Selection and Circularity:}
To minimize our manufacturing footprint, we have opted for 3D-printed components using \textbf{PLA}. PLA is a bio-based, industrially compostable polymer derived from renewable resources like corn starch. By using additive manufacturing, we significantly reduce material waste compared to traditional subtractive machining.

\vspace{1em}

\textbf{Life Cycle and Battery Management:}
We recognize that Lithium-Polymer (LiPo) batteries pose a chemical risk if disposed of improperly. Our operational plan includes a strict end-of-life protocol where degraded cells are discharged and taken to certified e-waste recycling centers. We have designed the electronics with standardized connectors to ensure that sensors and flight controllers can be harvested and reused in future iterations of the platform, extending the functional life of the high-value silicon components.

\vspace{1em}

\textbf{Social and Safety Impact:}
Sustainability includes the protection of human capital. FireFly enhances the safety of first responders by providing situational awareness in hazardous terrains without risking human life. By creating a rapidly deployable tool, we extend access to advanced fire-monitoring capability to smaller forestry divisions and academic researchers who operate outside the reach of satellite-based or manned aerial surveillance programs.

\vspace{1em}

\textbf{Ethics Statement:}
As engineers, we recognize that our work has a direct impact on public safety and the environment. We are committed to upholding the highest standards of honesty, transparency, and responsibility throughout the design and development of FireFly. Our system is intended solely to support fire prevention and responder safety. \subsection{Operational Scenarios for Wildfire Monitoring}
FireFly is designed to perform in three main scenarios to assist first responders quickly and ecological researchers in monitoring and analyzing active wildfire threats.

\vspace{1em}

\textbf{Active Fire Detection:}
FireFly will serve as a primary monitoring node in blind spot regions. These regions are areas where satellites and tower coverage is blocked due to topography. By monitoring real time feed from the camera, FireFly uses image recognition models to detect fire and smoke at a high accuracy. FireFly can verify ignition and smoke for early detection.

\vspace{1em}

\textbf{Fire Propagation and Heading Analysis:}
By utilizing real time calibrated camera data, FireFly can track the movement of a fire. By comparing sensor data such as increasing temperature gradients and changing smoke concentrations alongside the GPS, the system can determine the direction and speed at which a fire is heading as well as changes in intensity.

\vspace{1em}

\textbf{Perimeter Mapping and Size Assessment:}
FireFly can be deployed in multiple locations at the boundary of a detected fire. By triangulating the area of the fire, the ground station can plot a geofenced perimeter. This provides responders with an accurate estimate of total area affected in the terrain.

\subsection{Sensor}

\subsubsection{Intro}

While a drone equipped solely with a standard optical camera provides a valuable "eye in the sky," it is fundamentally insufficient for the complex task of early-phase wildfire detection and comprehensive situational awareness. Visual feeds are easily obscured by dense canopy, low-light conditions, or the very smoke the system is trying to identify, leaving first responders with an incomplete and potentially misleading picture of the threat. To provide the high-fidelity data required for rapid emergency response, a drone must move beyond simple sight and operate as a mobile, multi-modal sensing laboratory capable of "smelling" and "feeling" the environment long before flames become visible to the naked eye.


To bridge this information gap, our design integrates a sophisticated array of atmospheric and thermal hardware designed to cross-verify environmental anomalies. The onboard suite includes long-range infrared (IR) imaging to pierce through visual obstructions and identify heat signatures, alongside a chemical sensing core consisting of carbon monoxide (CO), carbon dioxide ($CO_2$), and volatile organic compound (VOC) sensors. By augmenting these with particulate matter (PM) sensors to track smoke density, the platform can distinguish between harmless atmospheric changes and active combustion events. This fused data approach ensures that the information relayed to ground crews is not just a visual confirmation, but a detailed chemical and thermal profile of the fire's behavior.

\vspace{1em}

\subsubsection{LWIR(Long-Wave Infrared)}

Long-Wave Infrared (LWIR) refers to a specific band of the electromagnetic spectrum, typically spanning wavelengths from $8 \mu m$ to $14 \mu m$. Unlike visible light, which is reflected off surfaces, LWIR sensors detect the thermal radiation emitted naturally by all objects with a temperature above absolute zero. In this "thermal" window, the atmosphere is remarkably transparent, allowing long-wave energy to travel through air with minimal absorption. This allows LWIR cameras to act as heat maps rather than traditional photographs, converting the invisible infrared energy of the environment into a visible image where intensity corresponds to temperature.


In the context of drone hardware, LWIR sensors are distinct from the "Near-Infrared" used in night-vision goggles or TV remotes. While Near-IR still relies on some light reflecting off an object, LWIR is completely passive and does not require any external light source to function. This makes it a "true" thermal imaging technology. Because it operates in a specific atmospheric window, it is uniquely suited for outdoor, long-range sensing where environmental factors like humidity and distance might otherwise scatter or block shorter wavelengths of energy.


The primary importance of LWIR in fire detection lies in its ability to achieve "visual penetration" through smoke and debris. In a wildfire, a standard camera is often blinded by the thick, opaque clouds of smoke produced by burning organic matter. However, the wavelengths of LWIR are significantly larger than the soot particles found in smoke, allowing the thermal radiation to pass through the plume virtually unimpeded. This enables the drone to "see" through the smoke to locate the actual seat of the fire, providing first responders with the exact coordinates of the ignition point rather than just the location of the smoke cloud.


Furthermore, LWIR is essential for identifying "incipient" or hidden fires that have not yet broken through the forest canopy. In many forest fires, the heat can build up on the ground or within the duff layer before flames are visually apparent from above. An LWIR sensor can detect these thermal anomalies—hotspots that are significantly warmer than the surrounding terrain—allowing for the discovery of potential fires before they escalate into massive conflagrations. This early detection window is the most critical period for containment efforts.


Finally, LWIR provides a level of quantitative data that visual cameras cannot offer. By utilizing radiometric LWIR sensors, the system can measure the actual temperature of the ground in real-time. This allows the drone to distinguish between a "false positive," such as a sun-warmed rock or a vehicle, and the intense thermal signature of active combustion. For first responders, this means the drone can provide a "fire line" map, showing exactly where the fire is most intense and where it has already cooled, which is vital for safely directing ground crews and aerial water drops.

\vspace{1em}

\subsubsection{Live Camera Feed}

A live camera feed serves as the primary visual confirmation layer, transforming abstract sensor data into actionable intelligence. While gas and thermal sensors can identify the presence of a fire, the optical feed allows operators and autonomous systems to verify the "ground truth" of the situation. This visual context is vital for distinguishing between a high-confidence fire event and environmental anomalies, such as a localized dust cloud or a reflection that might trigger a single-sensor alert. For first responders, seeing the actual environment provides an immediate understanding of the terrain, fuel types, and potential obstacles that no numerical data point can replicate.


By integrating this live feed into an onboard AI fire detection model, the system gains the ability to perform real-time pattern recognition and smoke characterization. Modern computer vision algorithms, such as convolutional neural networks, are trained to identify the specific volumetric expansion and grayish-white color of rising smoke plumes that often precede visible flames. Because the AI processes the video frame-by-frame as it is captured, it can detect the subtle motion of smoke against a static background far faster than a human operator might. This automation ensures that the drone remains a vigilant sentinel, constantly scanning the horizon for the first visual signs of ignition.


The synergy between the live feed and AI is also critical for the "locate" phase of the mission. By analyzing the visual data alongside the drone’s telemetry, the AI can estimate the fire's coordinates and movement vectors with high precision. For instance, the AI can track the "head" of the fire and the direction of the smoke drift, allowing the system to project the fire's probable path over the next few minutes. This real-time mapping is essential for protecting ground crews and determining which areas are at the highest risk of immediate spread, providing a level of foresight that is only possible through continuous visual monitoring.


Lastly, the live feed provides the essential "human-in-the-loop" capability required for high-stakes emergency response. Even with sophisticated AI, the ability for a remote incident commander to view the fire line in real-time allows for informed decision-making regarding the deployment of resources. The video feed can highlight specific hazards, such as nearby structures, power lines, or trapped vehicles, which are critical for tactical planning. Ultimately, the live feed acts as the bridge between autonomous detection and human intervention, ensuring that the information gathered by the drone is utilized to its fullest potential in saving lives and protecting property.

\vspace{1em}

\subsection{Communication}
\subsubsection{intro}
Detecting wildfires in vast, dense forests presents a critical challenge for emergency responders, as early detection is the only way to prevent a small brush fire from becoming an uncontrollable catastrophe. In these environments, a single drone is severely limited by its flight time, battery capacity, and sensor range; it simply cannot scan thousands of acres quickly enough to find a pinpoint heat source before it spreads. Furthermore, a lone drone can only provide a single "line of sight" to a fire, which makes it nearly impossible to determine the exact GPS coordinates or the actual size of the blaze from a distance. Relying on one unit creates a single point of failure where a mechanical issue or a loss of signal in a remote valley results in a total loss of monitoring capability.

To solve this, a mesh network of drones acts as a distributed "eye in the sky," turning a group of individual robots into a cohesive, intelligent sensing platform. By using multiple drones, the search area can be subdivided into a grid, allowing the team to cover a massive forest in a fraction of the time it would take a single unit. When a fire is spotted, the mesh allows multiple drones to converge and view the fire from different angles simultaneously. This multi-perspective data is sent back through the chain of drones—using each other as relays—to the ground station in the city. The central station then uses these intersecting vectors to triangulate the fire's precise location and calculate its growth rate with high accuracy, ensuring that firefighting resources are deployed exactly where they are needed most.

\subsubsection{Scout Relay Concept}

The primary obstacle in long-distance drone operations is the "line-of-sight" limitation of high-frequency radio signals. In a heavily forested or mountainous environment, physical obstacles like ridges, thick timber, and even the curvature of the Earth act as barriers that swallow data signals. If a single scout drone flies deep into a valley to investigate a heat signature, the terrain will likely sever its direct link to a distant city-based ground station. By strategically positioning relay drones between the scout and the base, the system creates a "daisy chain" that carries the signal over and around these obstacles, ensuring that the scout remains connected no matter how rugged the landscape becomes.

Furthermore, these relay points are essential for maintaining the high bandwidth required for real-time video and complex sensor data. As a radio signal travels, its strength decays rapidly over distance, leading to "noise" and data loss that can cause video feeds to freeze or telemetry to drop. By using a series of relay drones, the data only has to travel a short distance between each "hop." This keeps the signal strength high and the latency low, allowing the artificial intelligence at the ground station to receive a clear, high-definition stream. Without these intermediate points, the data would arrive corrupted or not at all, rendering the scout’s advanced fire-detection sensors useless.

Beyond just signal strength, the relay drones provide a critical layer of operational resilience and "self-healing" for the entire mission. In an active wildfire scenario, conditions change rapidly; smoke plumes can become dense enough to scatter radio waves, or a drone might need to move to avoid extreme heat. A multi-drone mesh allows the network to stay flexible; if one relay point needs to shift its position, the other drones can adjust their spacing to maintain the link. This cooperative behavior ensures a persistent data pipeline, allowing the scouts to focus entirely on the fire while the relays guarantee that the life-saving information they gather actually reaches the decision-makers in the city.

\subsubsection{MANET}

The planned Mobile Ad-hoc Network (MANET) is the "nervous system" that would transform a group of independent drones into a synchronized sensing fleet. Unlike traditional networks that rely on a fixed router or central tower, a MANET allows each drone to act as both receiver and transmitter. In the unpredictable environment of a forest fire, drones are constantly moving, banking, and changing altitudes to get a better view or to avoid smoke. The MANET layer is intended to manage these shifting positions in real time, automatically calculating the most efficient path for data to travel from the scout to the ground station through available relays.

The most important target characteristic of a MANET in this scenario is self-healing. In a wildfire mission, the risk of losing a drone to technical failure, low battery, or extreme environmental conditions is high. In a rigid network, the loss of a single relay drone can break the communication chain. A decentralized mesh should let the remaining drones reroute data through other nearby units as long as a physical path to the base remains.

Furthermore, MANET technology allows for dynamic scalability, which is essential when the scale of a fire grows unexpectedly. If a fire spreads and more drones are deployed from the city to assist, they can join the MANET instantaneously without needing a manual configuration or a reboot of the system. The network simply expands to include the new "nodes," automatically incorporating them into the relay chain or the search grid. This "plug-and-play" nature of ad-hoc networking allows emergency responders to scale their surveillance efforts in real-time as the situation on the ground evolves.

Finally, using a MANET is important because it reduces the "overhead" and interference that usually plagues large-scale wireless systems. By using intelligent routing protocols, the drones can "gossip" with their immediate neighbors to determine which path is the least congested or has the strongest signal. This prevents the airwaves from becoming cluttered with redundant transmissions, ensuring that the high-priority triangulation data and AI video feeds reach the ground station with minimal latency. In a life-or-death firefighting scenario, the milliseconds saved by an optimized MANET path can be the difference between catching a fire early and losing control of the forest.

\subsubsection{LoRa and COFDM}

LoRa, short for Long Range, is a foundational component of our communication strategy, utilizing a spread-spectrum modulation technique that allows for connectivity over extreme distances. Its primary strength lies in its "receive sensitivity," meaning it can maintain a link even when the signal is incredibly faint or obstructed by dense forest canopies. For emergency operations, this provides a nearly unbreakable "lifeline" for the most critical data points: the drone’s GPS coordinates, battery health, and high-priority fire detection alerts.

However, the physical laws of radio frequency dictate a strict trade-off between range and speed. Because LoRa is optimized for distance and low power consumption, it has a very narrow data bandwidth—equivalent to sending simple text strings rather than complex media. While it is the ultimate tool for ensuring we never lose track of a drone, it is fundamentally incapable of transmitting the high-definition video streams required for modern AI-driven analysis. If used in isolation, the system would be "aware" of a drone's location but "blind" to the visual details of the fire.

This limitation creates a significant hurdle for remote sensing: a mission that only provides coordinates without visual confirmation lacks the context needed for high-level decision-making. Investors must recognize that relying on a single radio protocol in a vast forest creates a bottleneck that renders advanced AI software useless. To bridge the gap between "long range" and "high quality," a more sophisticated, multi-layered approach is required to meet the demands of professional firefighting agencies.

This motivates the planned Split-Link Architecture. This design philosophy separates communication into two distinct "pipes" operating simultaneously. Rather than forcing a single radio to compromise on both speed and distance, the mature system would use specialized hardware for low-bandwidth telemetry and higher-bandwidth video. This dual-path strategy keeps the "nervous system" of the fleet--telemetry, GPS, bearing, and alert data--independent from the "eyes" of the fleet--the video feed.

For the future high-bandwidth path, the system evaluates COFDM (Coded Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing) because OFDM-style links can tolerate multipath better than simple single-carrier video links. In a forest, radio signals are reflected off trees and terrain, which can fragment video. A COFDM video path is therefore treated as a planned hardware upgrade for non-line-of-sight video, not as a capability demonstrated by the current repository.

In the current prototype, the video path is implemented with recorded fire videos, ESP32-CAM or MJPEG stream URLs, OpenCV frame capture, and YOLOv8 inference running on the ground-station computer. The proof-of-concept therefore validates detection and map posting, while future RF hardware must still be selected and tested for real outdoor range, bandwidth, latency, and multipath performance.

The Split-Link Architecture is intended to solve range and video quality as separate engineering problems. If the high-bandwidth video link degrades, the lower-bandwidth telemetry path should continue to carry GPS, heading, battery state, and fire-alert packets. This supports a "fail-functional" mission mode in which the operator may lose video detail but still retains the data required to track drone position and estimate fire location.

Ultimately, dual-protocol hardware is a future design direction for operational resilience. The implemented repository currently uses Wi-Fi UDP telemetry and HTTP APIs, while the MANET, LoRa, and COFDM portions remain requirements for the next hardware iteration.

\subsubsection{Automation Concept}
To maximize operational efficiency and ensure rapid response, the full FireFly concept uses a dynamic automation workflow that transitions through three distinct phases: the Lawnmower Search, the Relay Chain, and the Orbital Ring. This tiered approach is a design target for future autonomous flight software. The current repository simulates drone movement and validates how the ground station reacts to detection and telemetry data.

In the initial "Search Phase," the fleet executes a Lawnmower pattern, which is the industry standard for 100 percent visual coverage. The mission area is divided into precise parallel "lanes," and each drone is autonomously assigned a specific path. This systematic grid ensures that we are not just flying aimlessly, but are instead "scanning" the forest floor with mathematical certainty. By subdividing the area among multiple drones, we can search thousands of acres in minutes—a task that would take hours for a single aircraft or ground crew.

As the search expands farther from the ground station, the future system would transition into the Relay Chain configuration. Not all drones are sent to the front line; instead, automation software would position selected units as high-altitude signal repeaters. This creates a resilient data bridge for future video and telemetry links. This self-positioning relay logic is not currently implemented in the repository.

The moment a scout drone’s onboard AI detects a fire, the fleet enters the Orbital Ring phase. Rather than staying in a fixed grid, nearby drones are "called" to the detection site to surround the fire from multiple cardinal directions. This orbital formation is crucial for the triangulation math performed at the ground station. By viewing the heat source from three or four different angles simultaneously, the system can intersect those vectors to pinpoint the fire’s GPS coordinates and estimate its total acreage with far greater accuracy than a single observation ever could.

For the customer, this three-stage automation means the entire mission—from search to discovery to precise location tracking—is handled with minimal human intervention. The drones "decide" how to move based on the needs of the network and the urgency of the fire. This workflow provides a professional-grade solution that combines the speed of a broad search with the tactical precision of a localized orbital scan, ensuring that firefighting teams have the exact data they need the moment a fire begins to spread.

\subsection{Onboard Systems}

\subsubsection{Intro}
FireFly utilizes an autonomous patrol and respond logic designed to maximize coverage. This ensures data can be captured upon fire detection. During the initial search phase, the drone performs a preset pattern, typically a circular orbit or in parallel lines like a lawnmower at a constant altitude. This phase provides steady environmental monitoring, where the onboard systems are continuously sampling data from GPS coordinates, barometric altitude, and inertial measurements. The camera provides a live visual feed for detection and data training. By sweeping the area under a stabilized flight path, the system provides a baseline for specific geographical areas. This allows the ground station to differentiate between normal atmospheric variations and detected sharp spikes associated with wildfires.

\vspace{0.5em}

When the drone detects a fire triggered by an automated visual confirmation from server side detection, the drone shifts to a search state and begins tracking. The integrated camera utilizes the pretrained detection framework to identify smoke and flame signatures in real time providing an automated verification layer. The system immediately records telemetry data and sends a packet containing the drone's precise GPS coordinates (latitude, longitude, and altitude), direction, and specific sensor values. This data is transmitted to the ground station, where it is recorded. By utilizing the camera, operators at the base station can confirm the fire's existence, cut down on false positives, and assess the intensity before the drone begins the mapping sequence. 

\vspace{0.5em}

\subsubsection{Autonomy and Behavior}
% System is a state machine, path planning such as lawn mower or orbit, fail safes and obstacle avoidance
The flight logic is controlled by a state machine that transitions between search, engagement, and recovery. During the search phase, the system will utilize a Boustrophedon path planning algorithm, a "lawnmower" pattern, to ensure systematic coverage of a designated area with a small percentage of sensor overlap. If a fire is detected, the drone dynamically adjusts its flight path to maintain a safe standoff distance based on thermal intensity. In the recovery state, built-in fail safes include an automated return to home protocol, signal loss, and hardware anomalies. These can be triggered by low battery thresholds, loss of signal exceeding five seconds, or detected malfunctions in the propulsion system.

\vspace{0.5em}

\textbf{Search State:} In autonomous surveillance, the search state is the foundational operating mode where the system systematically monitors the geographic area to confirm a baseline and search for anomalies. The lawnmower pattern or a horizontal sweep pattern has several technical advantages essential for high reliability and wildfire detection.

The search state is characterized by steady state flight at a constant altitude and velocity. This stability is essential for the server side processing as it provides consistent video feed and minimizes motion blur. In this state, the drone operates on a preset navigation schedule, this means it follows a computed path until a higher priority command is received from the ground station like smoke or fire being detected. The horizontal sweep pattern ensures that every square meter of a designated area is scanned. By calculating the drone's altitude and the camera's field of view, the distance between parallel passes or "track spacing" can be set to ensure no gaps exist between each pass. For the server side architecture, sensor overlap is typically set to 20\% is used as a buffer against environmental variables. This overlap compensates for wind drift, altitude variations, and server inference time. In the case of wind drift, if a gust pushes the drone off the path, the overlap ensures the intended path is still captured. When the drone tilts to compensate for wind, the camera's ground footprint shifts, overlap covers the "blind spots" during the parallel passes. 

Since the ground station is receiving a continuous video stream for analysis, having a predictable, linear flight path simplifies the georeferencing process. When the server detects a flame, it can easily correlate a point on a straight line path than it could if the drone were to perform on erratic or curved movements. If a drone loses signal or experiences a hardware anomaly during its path, the relative position is easy to calculate. 

\vspace{0.5em}

\textbf{Engagement and Mapping State:} This phase represents the the shift of the drone from autonomous surveillance to dynamic track once a fire is confirmed. This transition is triggered by a high priority command sent from the ground station to the drone. Upon receiving this command with the verified coordinates, the drone immediately exits the preset Boustrophedon pattern. In this state, the drone begins a standoff orbit safely circling based on real time environmental factors. This protects the airframe from extreme heat and updrafts. During this mapping phase, the primary drone coordinates its path with secondary drones via a network. The secondary drones provide a specific angular offset relative to the primary drone and maintain synchronized speed and path avoiding the risk of collision.

\vspace{0.5em}

\textbf{Recovery State:} The recovery state is a high priority override mode to protect the drone and prevent crashes. The transition to this state is immediate and overrides any active search or mapping behavior. The flight architecture relies heavily on ground station analysis, because of this any interruption in the high bandwidth video or telemetry data link exceeding five continuous seconds triggers an immediate hover mode. If a link is not reestablished within thirty seconds, the drone switches to the recovery state. In cases of battery fail safes, a soft fail safe is triggered at a 30\% capacity threshold, this tell the drone to stop its mission and navigate to the home point. A critical threshold at 10\% triggers a hard fail safe, this forces the drone to conduct a controlled vertical descent to protect the propulsion system from power loss. When the recovery state is engaged under normal conditions, the drone will ascend and return. It first ascends vertically to a clearance altitude absent of local terrain and forest canopy. Once the drone reaches a safe altitude, the drone will navigate a direct linear path via GPS back to the initial takeoff coordinates, it will then perform a descent and disable the motors upon touchdown.

\vspace{0.5em}

\subsubsection{Vision Detection}
% Describe how the server analyzes frames to detect fire/smoke. Verification logic: explain how to avoid false positives
% Confidence scoring of data. 
The system's perception layer is built upon a deep learning, one stage object detection model optimized between inference speed and average precision. The architecture is adept at real time forest fire detection due to its feature extraction. This allows for high confidence identification of small scale smoke plumes and high luminosity flame signatures from high altitude aerial feeds.
The vision pipeline is integrated into a server side flask server that manages the live video stream transmitted from the drone. Upon receiving the frames, the inference model performs classifications to distinguish between active fire and smoke particulate. To ensure reliability and moderate false positives caused by environmental factors such as lens flares, solar reflections, or thick dust the system utilizes a confidence scoring mechanism.
A detection is only posted if the model maintains a confidence score above a calibrated threshold across a defined window of consecutive frames. This verification logic prevents the system from triggering alerts on visual noise. Once the system detects the threshold is met, it executes a warning protocol. A signal is sent to drone to transition states from search to engagement and mapping. The incident is timestamped and logged with telemetry data.

\vspace{0.5em}
\subsubsection{Triangulation}
% math behind how the "map" is created, how drones orbit/circle fire to make points, talk about algorithms to connect coordinates into a polygon, area estimation formulas
The system uses a geometric triangulation method to calculate the precise coordinates of a fire’s center point by intersecting bearing rays from multiple drones. This approach allows the swarm to pinpoint a fire’s location from a safe standoff distance without requiring the drone to fly directly over the thermal source. By collecting directional vectors from two or more drones, the ground station can calculate a single high fidelity GPS coordinate.

The system first transforms the spherical GPS coordinates (latitude and longitude) of each drone into a Cartesian plane (x,y) measured in meters. This top down approximation allows the software to treat the drones’ positions as origin points for directional rays. Each unit reports its current position and a calculated heading toward the perceived center of the fire based on visual and thermal sensor alignment.The intersection point is determined by solving for the crossing of these rays. The ground station computes direction vectors for each drone based on its reported heading. The intersection representing the fire’s center is found by calculating the point where the rays most closely converge. The algorithm will discard rays when they are considered parallel or near parallel, indicating insufficient angular separation for accurate calculation. The system requires a positive scalar multiplier for the intersection, verifying that the fire is located in the forward view of the sensors rather than the projected behind of the drone.

While the center can be estimated with a single pair of drones, the design supports an N-vector model where data from additional units can increase the confidence level. By utilizing multiple vectors, the server can apply a weighted average of various intersection points. This reduces the impact of individual sensor noise or atmospheric interference, such as smoke obscuring the view from a specific angle. The triangulation logic is synchronized with the drones’ telemetry data. This allows the server to correlate vectors even if the drones are moving at different velocities because the bearing rays are timestamped.

\vspace{0.5em}
\subsubsection{Telemetry and Data}
% What data is sent back and organized for ground station, spatial data: lat/long, list of coords to form ongoing perimeter, system status such as signal strength, battery. Priority Queueing of data, sending detectin alerts before raw video feed, etc.
The communication architect of the platform is defined by a bidirectional data link between the drones and the ground station. This link allows for real time transmission of video feed, sensor collection, and system health metrics to the server, while simultaneously delivering mission commands and state transition instructions to the drone.
\vspace{0.5em}

\textbf{Downlink: Drone to Ground Station}: The downlink is responsible for providing the ground station with the necessary environmental and operational data to execute the mission and safety protocols.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Primary Visual Stream:} A high definition, low latency video feed is the central component of the downlink. This video stream is transmitted to the ground station to be processed by the server side vision engine. Since the detection and classification of fire and smoke occur on the server, the integrity and continuity of this feed are essential for alerting the system’s warning protocols.
  \item \textbf{Spatial and Navigational Data:} The drone broadcasts precise GPS coordinates, barometric altitude, and inertial measurements. 
  \item \textbf{System Health:} Real time monitoring of the various hardware and software modules including propulsion system health, power management system, and communication integrity. The propulsion system is the most critical for maintaining flight. Monitoring focuses on the physical components that generate lift. Maintaining stable power is vital for both the flight controller, motors, and communication. The signal strength is important in maintaining proper bitrate and latency of data being sent back to the ground station.
  
\end{itemize}
\vspace{0.5em}

\textbf{Uplink: Ground Station to Drone}: The uplink serves as the command and control channel, this allows the ground station to update the drone’s mission based on the analysis of incoming video video. The packets being sent prioritizes state transition commands, dynamic waypoint updates, and failsafe overrides. The state transition commands sent from the ground station command the drone to exit a search state and enter a mapping and triangulation procedure. The uplink allows for real time adjustment of the flight path, such as providing coordinates for the offset orbit or a new search domain. In the case of a failsafe, manual or automated recovery commands are sent to ensure the safety of the drone.
\vspace{0.5em}

\textbf{Priority Data Management}

To ensure reliability in remote environments where signal bandwidth may fluctuate, the communication firmware utilizes a priority based queuing protocol. Mission critical data is categorized into levels of urgency to prevent bottlenecks. Critical alerts are prioritized first including, detection notifications, battery failsafe triggers, and manual override commands. These are low bandwidth packets and are transmitted with high redundancy to ensure delivery even under signal degradation.
Video feed is categorized as mission critical data. The system relies on server side analysis, the video stream acts as the primary data source for detection logic and triangulation. The high bandwidth stream ensures the ground station receives the visual input required to maintain active monitoring. By pairing the stream with synchronized spatial telemetry, the design allows the server to process real time visual evidence without latency. Lastly, Navigation and Health systems cover GPS updates and hardware health metrics. They are sent at a consistent frequency to maintain operational function. 

\subsection{Server} 
\subsubsection{Intro}
Now that we have a drone equipped with a sophisticated sensor suite and a robust communication infrastructure to transfer that data, we require a centralized intelligence to process that raw information and transform it into actionable insights for first responders. The server acts as the command-of-operations center, serving as the bridge between the high-altitude hardware and the human decision-makers on the ground. Without a powerful backend to aggregate, analyze, and interpret the incoming streams of telemetry and video, the system would merely be a collection of disconnected data points rather than a cohesive life-saving tool.
Operating as the "digital brain" of the FireFly system, the server is responsible for the heavy computational lifting that exceeds the capacity of edge microcontrollers. By centralizing complex tasks such as real-time computer vision analysis, spatial triangulation, and fleet management, the server ensures that the data is not only stored but is actively interrogated for patterns that indicate fire behavior. This centralized architecture allows for a "single source of truth," where every piece of chemical and thermal data is fused together to provide a comprehensive, real-time map of the emergency landscape.

\vspace{0.5em}
\subsubsection{Drone Management}
The drone management system serves as the central conductor for the multi-node network, maintaining a comprehensive, real-time registry of every "Scout" and "Relay" unit in the field. The server continuously monitors a telemetry stream from each individual drone, tracking critical flight metrics such as remaining battery voltage, GPS coordinates, and signal strength within the MANET. By aggregating the live sensor data from each unit into a single dashboard, the server ensures that the health and performance of the entire fleet are visible at a glance. This centralized oversight allows the system to predict when a drone might need to return for a battery swap or when a relay node needs to shift position to maintain a stable link.
When a scout drone successfully identifies a fire through its onboard AI and sensor fusion logic, the server shifts from a monitoring state into an active tactical coordinator. Upon a high-confidence detection, the server utilizes the drone’s triangulated coordinates to establish a "target zone." It then issues autonomous commands to nearby drones in the mesh, dynamically reassigning their flight paths to converge on the incident area. By managing these flight trajectories centrally, the server ensures that multiple drones can transition from a broad search pattern to a concentrated surveillance formation without the risk of mid-air collisions.
This coordinated "surrounding" maneuver is designed to provide first responders with a 360-degree view of the fire’s perimeter. As the drones relocate, the server manages their positioning to create a visual and chemical "containment ring," where different units monitor the fire's head, flanks, and tail simultaneously. This multi-angle perspective is vital for tracking the fire’s rate of spread and direction in real-time. By automating this swarm behavior, the drone management system removes the burden of manual piloting from the ground crew, allowing them to focus entirely on the tactical information being delivered by the newly formed surveillance perimeter.

\vspace{0.5em}
\subsubsection{History}
As the FireFly system generates a constant stream of high-resolution telemetry and visual data, it becomes essential to maintain a comprehensive historical log of every incoming metric. For first responders, the value of this data lies in the ability to establish a "baseline" for the specific environment being monitored. By archiving standard readings of atmospheric gases, humidity, and thermal patterns during normal conditions, the server provides a critical point of comparison. When an anomaly occurs, the system doesn't just report a number; it can demonstrate how significantly the current environment has deviated from the norm, allowing for a much more accurate assessment of the threat level.
To ensure this data remains accessible and actionable, the server utilizes a dedicated time-series database designed for high-frequency logging. This storage architecture is optimized to handle the heavy throughput of real-time sensor updates while allowing for rapid querying of past events. Every data point—from the specific parts-per-million of Carbon Monoxide to the GPS coordinates of a scout drone—is time-stamped and indexed, creating a "black box" record of the entire mission. This historical archive not only powers the real-time trend graphs on the front end but also serves as an invaluable resource for post-incident analysis, helping fire investigators reconstruct the fire's origin and behavior with scientific precision.

\vspace{0.5em}
\subsubsection{AI}
To process the visual data streams, the server utilizes two distinct AI agents, each specialized in a specific wavelength of the electromagnetic spectrum. The first agent is trained exclusively on an extensive dataset of optical (RGB) imagery, comprising thousands of examples of forest environments in various states—including clear weather, heavy fog, and active fire scenarios. By training the model on "negative" samples (forests without fire) alongside "positive" samples (smoke and flames), the agent learns to identify the visual textures and volumetric patterns of combustion while ignoring common environmental distractions like cloud cover or light reflections.
The second AI agent is specifically tuned for Long-Wave Infrared (LWIR) imagery. Unlike the optical agent, this model is trained to recognize radiometric heat signatures and thermal gradients rather than colors or shapes. The training set for this agent includes a wide array of thermal profiles, ranging from the natural heat of sun-warmed rocks and animals to the intense, localized "thermal spikes" characteristic of an ignition point. Because infrared data can appear abstract to the human eye, this specialized agent is essential for filtering out background noise and identifying concentrated heat sources that may be obscured by trees or light brush.
By running these two agents in parallel, the server can cross-reference the visual world with the thermal world in real-time. For instance, if the optical agent detects what looks like a plume of smoke, the server immediately queries the infrared agent to see if there is a corresponding heat signature at the base of that plume. This "dual-vision" verification significantly reduces the likelihood of false positives. If both agents report a positive detection simultaneously, the system can confirm the presence of a fire with a much higher degree of certainty than any single-camera system could provide.
This visual intelligence is critical because it provides a qualitative "eyes-on" perspective that complements the quantitative data from the chemical sensors. While a gas sensor might tell the server that Carbon Monoxide levels are rising, the AI agents can show whether that gas is coming from a localized campfire or a rapidly expanding wildfire line. By analyzing the shape and spread of the pixels identified as "fire" or "smoke," the agents provide the server with a sense of the fire's scale and intensity, allowing the system to categorize the threat level visually before a human operator even looks at the feed.
Ultimately, these agents function as a high-speed filtration system that distills hours of video footage into a few seconds of critical information. Instead of requiring first responders to monitor multiple screens constantly, the AI agents highlight only the frames where anomalies are detected, drawing bounding boxes or heat maps around the areas of concern. This ensures that the visual information relayed to the front end is not just a raw video feed, but an intelligent, interpreted map of the fire's presence, providing ground crews with a clear, visual understanding of exactly what they are up against.

\vspace{0.5em}
\subsubsection{Confidence}
The final stage of the server’s analytical pipeline is the generation of a dynamic confidence rating, a multi-layered scoring system that translates raw data into a reliable threat assessment. Each individual data stream—from the LWIR AI’s visual patterns to the concentration ratios of $CO_2$ and Carbon Monoxide—is assigned its own "Category Confidence" score. For instance, if the infrared AI detects a concentrated heat signature, it may generate a 90 percent confidence rating for that specific channel. Simultaneously, the chemical sensors evaluate their own readings against historical baselines; a sharp spike in VOCs and Particulate Matter would produce a high chemical confidence score. By evaluating each category independently, the system ensures that no single sensor can trigger a false alarm, requiring a consensus across different physical and chemical domains.
Once these individual category ratings are established, the server utilizes a weighted aggregation algorithm to produce a unified "Final Confidence Rating." This master score is not a simple average; instead, the system prioritizes data points that are most characteristic of a wildfire. For example, a simultaneous high-confidence hit from both the LWIR AI and the Carbon Monoxide sensor carries significantly more weight than a spike in Particulate Matter alone, which could be caused by non-fire events like wind-blown dust. This hierarchical weighting allows the server to filter out environmental noise and provide first responders with a single, clear percentage that represents the statistical probability of an active, dangerous fire.
This Final Confidence Rating is a living metric, designed to change dynamically as the drone's position and the environment evolve. As the Scout drones converge on the triangulated source and more sensors begin to report from different angles, the server continuously re-evaluates the score. If the $CO_2$ levels continue to climb while the AI confirms the presence of an expanding smoke plume, the confidence rating will trend toward 100 percent. Conversely, if the heat signature dissipates or the gas concentrations stabilize, the system will automatically downgrade the alert level. This real-time adaptability ensures that first responders are always acting on the most current and verified intelligence, allowing for tactical decisions that shift as rapidly as the fire itself.

\subsection{Conceptualisations}

FireFly is a lightweight quadcopter designed to collect and transmit
environmental sensor data in remote areas with no existing infrastructure.
The system is built around three functions: stable flight, environmental
sensing, and real-time data transmission.

The drone carries an onboard microcontroller paired with a temperature and humidity sensor and a smoke detector. A flight controller manages propulsion and stabilization while the onboard microcontroller handles sensor acquisition and wireless transmission. A LiPo battery powers both propulsion and electronics for the duration of a monitoring mission.

\vspace{0.5em}

\subsection{Brainstorming}
Several frame geometries were evaluated including X and H-style quadcopter
layouts. An X-frame was selected for its balanced motor distribution,
stability, and ease of sensor mounting.

\vspace{0.5em}

The sensing approach was inspired by existing ground-based forest sensor
networks, which are geographically limited and difficult to scale into remote
terrain. FireFly was conceived as a mobile alternative: a drone that could
collect the same environmental data in areas where fixed infrastructure does
not exist.

\vspace{0.5em}

For communication, Bluetooth Low Energy and Wi-Fi were both evaluated. A connectionless peer-to-peer wireless protocol was ultimately selected for its simplicity, low latency, and sufficient range for a point-to-point link between the drone and ground receiver without the overhead of a full Wi-Fi stack.

\vspace{0.5em}

Power and propulsion options were compared using eCalc to evaluate
thrust-to-weight ratio, hover current, and projected flight time across
different motor, propeller, and LiPo configurations. This guided selection

electrical load of the sensor and communication hardware.

\section{Front End}

\subsection{Website}
While the FireFly system operates with a high degree of technical sophistication, the vast streams of atmospheric chemistry and thermal imagery are functionally useless if the first responders cannot access and interpret them in the heat of an emergency. The front end serves as the vital translation layer, transforming complex raw telemetry and AI-driven detections into a clear, intuitive, and high-stakes user interface. By centralizing every facet of the mission into a unified web-based command center, the front end ensures that incident commanders are not overwhelmed by data, but are instead empowered with the situational awareness needed to make life-saving tactical decisions in real time. 

\subsection{Table Data}
The data management interface is centered around a multi-tier table that serves as the primary health and status monitor for the entire drone fleet. To ensure that incident commanders can make split-second decisions without being overwhelmed by raw numbers, the default "Processed" view utilizes a high-visibility color-coding system. Key metrics such as battery life and mesh connection strength are represented by a "traffic light" protocol—where green indicates optimal status, yellow warns of approaching thresholds, and red signals a critical failure or the need for immediate extraction. Rather than displaying complex chemical PPM (parts per million) values in this view, the table translates sensor readings into a simplified confidence rating for each drone, allowing an operator to instantly see which units have the highest probability of being near an active fire.
For technical leads or analysts who require deeper insight, the table features a global toggle to shift from processed ratings into "Raw Data" mode. When activated, the color-coded indicators are supplemented or replaced by the exact numerical values for Carbon Monoxide, $CO_2$, VOCs, and Particulate Matter. This transparency is vital for verifying the nuances of a fire's chemical signature or troubleshooting a specific sensor's behavior in the field. By providing both a simplified and a granular view, the website accommodates different levels of operational needs, from high-level strategic oversight to detailed environmental diagnostics.
To further enhance usability, the interface supports a "Drill-Down" capability that allows users to isolate a specific drone from the rest of the fleet. By clicking on an individual unit, such as "Scout 1," the table filters out all other distractions and expands a dedicated profile for that specific node. This isolated view provides a focused look at that drone’s specific sensor trends, precise GPS telemetry, and hardware health over time. This functionality is particularly important during a "Surround" maneuver, as it allows the commander to monitor the specific unit that is closest to the fire's head without losing sight of the broader mission data.

\subsection{History}
The history section of the website provides a comprehensive longitudinal record of the mission, presenting the recorded atmospheric data in a series of organized tables and trend graphs. This archival view specifically tracks the fluctuations of Carbon Monoxide, Carbon Dioxide, Volatile Organic Compounds, and Particulate Matter over the duration of the drone's flight, allowing responders to identify exactly when and where a chemical spike occurred. By maintaining this accessible log of historical data, the front end enables users to cross-reference current readings with past environmental baselines, turning a momentary sensor spike into a documented trend that can be used to map the fire's progression or verify its eventual suppression.

\subsection{Live Feed}
The website's live feed interface provides a side-by-side visual command center that streams high-definition optical video alongside Long-Wave Infrared (LWIR) imagery in real-time. This dual-pane layout is engineered for simultaneous verification, allowing incident commanders to overlay visual cues, such as smoke color and terrain features, with the corresponding thermal heat map. To enhance the utility of these feeds, the interface includes interactive overlays where the server-side AI highlights detected hotspots and smoke boundaries directly onto the video. This synchronized display ensures that visual obstructions—like thick smoke or a dense forest canopy—do not prevent the operator from maintaining a clear understanding of the fire’s precise location and intensity.

\subsection{Map}
The interactive GIS map acts as the primary spatial command center, providing a bird's-eye view of the entire operational theater. Each drone in the fleet is represented by a dynamic icon that moves across the map in real-time, allowing incident commanders to track the exact distribution and search patterns of the Scouts and Relays. To maintain clarity, each icon is labeled with the drone's unique ID and color-coded to match the "health" status seen in the data tables, ensuring that the commander can see not just where a drone is, but how it is performing relative to its position in the mission area.
When the server-side logic confirms a combustion event, the map automatically generates a high-visibility fire icon at the precise GPS coordinates determined by the triangulation algorithm. This icon serves as the tactical focal point for the entire mission, allowing ground crews to visualize the fire's location in relation to landmarks, access roads, and existing drones. By displaying the distance between the fire icon and the nearest Scout units, the map facilitates the "Surround" maneuver, providing a clear visual representation of the containment perimeter as it forms around the detected hazard.

\subsection{Final confidence rating}
The final element of the web interface is a dedicated "Global Confidence" dashboard, which serves as the primary diagnostic tool for the entire mission. Rather than focusing on individual drones, this page aggregates the processing results from every active node in the field to present a unified "Final Confidence Rating" at the top of the display. Below this master score, the dashboard breaks down the intelligence into two primary supporting pillars: the Vision-Based Confidence (derived from the collective data of the RGB and LWIR AI agents) and the Atmospheric Confidence (calculated from the fused chemical ratios of $CO_2$, CO, VOCs, and PM sensors). This top-down view provides the incident commander with a high-level summary of the system’s certainty, allowing them to see instantly if the chemical evidence is corroborating the visual detections across the entire operation area. 

\subsection{Security}
To ensure the integrity of the mission and the safety of the data, the FireFly website implements a robust, role-based security architecture centered around individual station authentication. Each firefighting district or command post is assigned a unique organizational account, requiring a secure login to access the dashboard. By default, the system enforces strict data compartmentalization: a station only has visibility and control over the specific drones and sensor nodes registered to their local fleet. This prevents cross-talk or accidental command interference between neighboring districts, ensuring that an incident commander in one county cannot inadvertently alter the flight path or settings of a drone operating in a different jurisdiction.
While the system is built on a foundation of privacy, it includes a "Collaborative Handshake" protocol that allows for the secure sharing of intelligence during large-scale, multi-agency responses. If a wildfire crosses jurisdictional lines, an authorized station lead can manually grant a "Share Permission" to another verified account, providing them with real-time access to their live feeds, sensor tables, and triangulation data. This sharing mechanism is designed to be granular and temporary; the host station can choose exactly which data layers to reveal and can revoke access instantly once the joint operation is concluded. This balance of rigid security and flexible cooperation ensures that while the drones are protected from unauthorized access, the flow of life-saving information remains unhindered during a crisis.

\subsection{Production}
To scale the FireFly project from a prototype to a widespread environmental solution, the project plan includes the establishment of a dedicated manufacturing facility designed for high-precision systematic assembly. This factory will utilize an optimized production line specifically tailored for the integration of sensitive electronic components, such as the Long-Wave Infrared (LWIR) sensors and custom gas sensor arrays. By implementing standardized manufacturing protocols and automated quality control testing at each stage of the assembly—from the soldering of the communication modules to the calibration of the flight controllers—the facility ensures that every drone that leaves the line meets the rigorous engineering standards required for emergency response hardware.
The production infrastructure is built on a modular "Scalable Factory" model, allowing the manufacturing capacity to expand dynamically in response to market demand. As more fire departments and forestry agencies adopt the technology, the facility can introduce additional assembly cells and shift from low-volume batch production to high-output continuous manufacturing without requiring a complete redesign of the workspace. This scalability is supported by a robust supply chain strategy that maintains strategic reserves of critical components, ensuring that during active wildfire seasons or periods of rapid agency expansion, the production rate can be accelerated to put more eyes in the sky exactly when they are needed most.

\subsection{Distro}
The distribution of the FireFly system will be managed through a centralized, professional e-commerce platform that serves as the primary gateway for government agencies and private fire services to acquire the technology. This dedicated online portal is designed to streamline the procurement process, allowing fire departments to customize their fleet configurations—selecting the number of Scout and Relay units—and manage their software subscriptions in one place. By bypassing traditional third-party retail channels, the direct-to-agency online model ensures that the hardware remains cost-effective while maintaining a direct line of communication between our engineering team and the end users for technical support and software updates.
To reach our target market effectively, the sales strategy focuses on building relationships with regional fire bureaus and environmental protection agencies through a "Regional Deployment" model. The website will feature a consultation portal where incident commanders can request a needs-based assessment, ensuring they receive the correct mix of sensors and communication hardware for their specific terrain, whether it be dense forest or open brushland. This digital storefront also serves as the hub for global distribution, coordinating the logistics of shipping sensitive hardware from the factory to the station house, while providing all necessary documentation, training manuals, and certification records required for immediate deployment.
Once a sale is finalized, the distribution website transitions into a long-term fleet management tool for the customer. Through their secure account, agencies can track the delivery of new hardware, order replacement sensor modules, and download the latest AI firmware updates. By housing the entire sales and distribution lifecycle within a single online ecosystem, we can ensure that every FireFly drone in the field is properly registered and supported. This model not only simplifies the initial purchase but also creates a scalable, global distribution network that can rapidly supply fire-prone regions around the world with the tools they need to combat emerging wildfire threats.

\subsection{Aesthetic Prototype}

The FireFly aesthetic prototype follows a field-ready design language: a compact enclosed central body with rounded corners, symmetric X-arm geometry, and cylindrical motor pods at each corner. The overall form emphasizes structural clarity and quick visual inspection rather than cosmetic styling. Rounded exterior edges reduce snag points during transport, while the open-top bay and side windows communicate where electronics, wiring, and battery hardware are intended to be serviced.

\subsection{Design for Manufacture, Assembly, Maintenance}

\textbf{Design for Manufacture (DFM):}
The current frame is designed as a single-piece 3D print to minimize part count and remove alignment errors. The v5 CAD envelope is approximately $100 \times 72 \times 24$ mm with $2.5$ mm floor and wall thicknesses, balancing print speed, stiffness, and mass. Arms are sized at $10.0$ mm width and $5.4$ mm thickness to maintain rigidity under motor thrust and landing loads. Motor pods are dimensioned for SunnySky R1106-class motors with built-in tolerance ($19$ mm outer diameter, $15.0$ mm inner diameter, $17$ mm height, $2.2$ mm mounting holes on a $9$ mm bolt-circle).

\vspace{0.5em}

\textbf{Design for Assembly (DFA):}
Assembly features are integrated directly into the print geometry. The frame includes four battery strap slots in the floor and continuous wire-routing paths from motor pods into the electronics bay. This allows a straightforward assembly order: install motors in pods, route motor leads through arm channels, mount the controller board, then secure the battery and sensor harness. Because most interfaces are geometric rather than adhesive, rework is possible without damaging the frame.

\vspace{0.5em}

\textbf{Design for Maintenance (DFMn):}
Maintenance access is provided by the open top cavity, side-wall windows, and pod-to-arm wire notches. These features allow quick inspection of solder joints, connector seating, and wire wear without full teardown. Electronics can be removed independently from propulsion hardware, and battery straps can be replaced in the field with standard hook-and-loop straps. In case of structural damage, the one-piece architecture supports rapid replacement by reprinting the frame using the same CAD file, preserving dimensional repeatability across repairs.

\subsection{Technology (Languages, Hardware, etc.)}

The FireFly system utilizes a custom-integrated hardware architecture designed for high-reliability telemetry in extreme environments. Rather than utilizing external third-party modules, the drone features a custom-designed Flight Control and Sensing PCB. This board integrates a high-performance 32-bit microcontroller with a dedicated RF front-end optimized for the 2.4GHz spectrum. The onboard sensor suite is deeply integrated into the hardware logic, utilizing an I2C-based sensor bus to interface custom environmental monitoring circuits—specifically high-precision thermistor arrays and particulate matter sensors—directly with the primary processing unit.

\vspace{0.5em}

The telemetry link is powered by a proprietary implementation of a low-latency peer-to-peer radio protocol, achieving sub-10ms latency and a 400-meter line-of-sight range. The ground station is a custom-fabricated receiver node that serves as a high-speed bridge between the RF environment and the data processing layer. It parses structured telemetry packets into a JSON-formatted stream via a native USB-to-Serial interface. The software stack is built for modularity and speed: embedded firmware is written in C for direct hardware control, while the backend infrastructure utilizes a Python-based micro-framework to feed a real-time JavaScript dashboard. This vertical integration—from custom silicon layout to the application layer—ensures maximum control over power consumption and data integrity.

\subsection{Simulations}

Before building and flying, we used simulation tools to verify that the FireFly configuration can sustain flight, carry its sensor payload, and meet the core mission requirement of delivering real-time environmental data during the pre-fire window. An online multicopter calculator (eCalc xcopterCalc) was used to evaluate combinations of frame size, motors, propellers, and battery against thrust-to-weight ratio, hover throttle, current draw, and expected flight time under sensor payload assumptions. This confirmed that the selected hardware provides sufficient thrust margin to carry the temperature/humidity sensor and smoke detector while maintaining acceptable hover efficiency and a flight time long enough to collect and transmit meaningful readings to the ground station.

\vspace{0.5em}

A simplified quadcopter dynamics model was used to tune attitude and altitude control parameters, verifying that the platform can hold a stable position over a monitoring area, a prerequisite for consistent sensor sampling. Research on quadcopter modeling confirms that such simulations can predict controller behavior and reduce the number of risky test flights needed to achieve stable flight. Together, these simulations provide evidence that FireFly can meet its need statement requirements: reaching areas with zero sensor infrastructure and sustaining flight long enough to collect and transmit temperature, humidity, and smoke data before committing to full implementation and field testing.
